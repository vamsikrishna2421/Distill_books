# Dances with Foes: How to Actually Win a Debate

Fifth night, friend — and tonight a human being steps onto a San Francisco stage to argue against a supercomputer.

February 2019. IBM's **Project Debater**: an AI trained on some *400 million newspaper articles*, built to win arguments. Across the stage: Harish Natarajan, holder of the record for most international debate victories — a man with fifteen minutes to prepare and exactly one brain. The motion: should preschool be subsidized? The machine argues yes; Harish argues no. The audience votes before and after — whoever moves more minds wins.

The machine has four hundred million articles. And Harish is about to win *decisively* — using almost none.

## The dance

![A lone debater at a podium facing a towering monolithic AI machine on a dramatic spotlit stage before a large audience](/story-art/think-again/05-debater.jpg)

Watch what each side did, friend, because the contrast is the whole lesson. The machine did what most of us do in an argument: it **buried the opponent in facts** — statistics, studies, reason after reason — persuasion as a war of attrition. Harish did the opposite. He opened by *agreeing*: preschool genuinely benefits children, and he wasn't disputing it. Then he reframed the real question — not whether preschool is *good*, but whether subsidies are the best use of scarce funds, since they often help the families who least need help. And instead of contesting every point, he **conceded the reasonable ones** and concentrated his fire on one or two decisive weaknesses. Conceding a valid point doesn't make you look weak, friend — it makes you look *credible and fair-minded*, which makes your remaining arguments land twice as hard.

Grant's larger image: a good debate is not a war, and not even a tug-of-war you win by pulling harder. It's an **unchoreographed dance** with a partner who has a different set of steps in mind. Lead too forcefully and the partner resists. Adapt — find rhythm in the agreements, step back where they have a point — and you can move *together* somewhere new. The prosecutor triggers defensiveness. The curious scientist invites the other side to rethink *with* you.

## What the experts actually do

Researcher Neil Rackham wanted to know what separates *elite* negotiators from merely experienced ones — so he identified experts by track record (rated effective by both sides, few deals that later blew up) and recorded over a hundred live negotiations.

::: guess
Four differences separated the experts from the average. One involved how they planned, one involved how MANY arguments they made, one involved questions. Take a guess: did experts make more arguments or fewer? More questions or fewer?
---
**Fewer arguments — and more than double the questions.** The four differences, friend: (1) Experts *planned for common ground* — roughly a **third** of their preparation went to finding areas of possible agreement, while average negotiators scripted battle plans. (2) Experts made **fewer arguments** — because of **argument dilution**: a single compelling reason is *stronger* than that reason plus three mediocre ones, since a savvy counterpart attacks the weakest link and the whole case starts to feel flimsy. Every weak reason you add is a gift to the other side. (3) Experts *asked questions* — over a **fifth** of everything they said, more than twice the average rate. "So you don't see any merit in this proposal at all?" — curiosity that surfaces real interests and invites reconsideration without commanding it. (4) Experts refused the **defend–attack spiral** — the escalating loop where each side dismisses and hardens. Instead of counterpunching they got curious, and they narrated their own feelings out loud: *"I'm confused by that reaction — can you help me understand it?"* The through-line, friend: hold your position a little more loosely, hold the relationship a little more tightly. **Fewer, better reasons; more, better questions.**
:::

## The vaccine whisperer

Now the hardest version of the problem: someone who isn't debating you at all — they've simply *decided*, and every fact you offer makes them dig in deeper. Our instinct is what psychologists call the **righting reflex** — fix, correct, lecture. It backfires almost every time, because pressure to change is an attack on autonomy, and people defend their autonomy like territory.

**Motivational interviewing** — developed by William Miller from his work with problem drinkers, with Stephen Rollnick — starts from the opposite premise: *you can rarely motivate someone else to change, but you can help them find their own motivation.* Three practices, friend: ask **open-ended questions** ("What would vaccinating look like for you?" — never "Don't you know vaccines are safe?"); practice **reflective listening** — restate what you heard, test it, show you grasp their concerns *before* offering anything; and **affirm their freedom to choose**, out loud. The engine is the difference between *sustain talk* (defending the status quo) and **change talk** (any flicker of desire, ability, reason, or need to change). Skilled interviewers never argue with sustain talk — but the moment change talk appears, they ask elaborating questions: *why would you want that? how might you do it?* Because hearing **your own voice** make the case is more convincing than hearing anyone else's — your own words can't threaten your autonomy.

::: guess
Quebec neonatologist Arnaud Gagneur put this into practice in maternity wards — one conversation, under an hour, with mothers hesitant about vaccines. No lecturing, no correcting; just open questions, reflection, and "whatever you decide, I respect your judgment as a parent." What happened to vaccination rates?
---
In his first study, a single conversation raised mothers' *intention* to vaccinate by about **15 percent**. Then the randomized trial across maternity wards: one session made children roughly **9 percentage points more likely to be fully vaccinated two years later** — a staggering return on twenty-odd minutes of listening. They called him **"the vaccine whisperer,"** and Quebec expanded the program. Grant tells one mother's story: Marie-Hélène Étienne-Rousseau, firmly opposed, her premature son Tobie in the hospital for months. The counselor asked about her fears, acknowledged the flood of conflicting information parents drown in, and closed by affirming the choice was hers. Before leaving the hospital, Tobie was vaccinated — and she later said what made the difference was *the feeling of being heard, not pressured*. Note the mechanism, friend: the counselors never told mothers they were wrong and never withheld facts — when parents asked, they answered. But the facts came **after** understanding, inside respect, alongside explicit freedom. And one ethical bright line: MI is a *mirror*, not a lever. Used to help people reach their own goals, it's motivational interviewing. Used to steer them toward yours, it's coercion in a nicer coat.
:::

## Mummy and the warlord

The extreme test: the early 1990s, when Ugandan minister **Betty Bigombe** was assigned the task the army had failed at with guns — ending Joseph Kony's brutal Lord's Resistance Army insurgency.

She began by *listening*. Months in camps and villages, asking rebels and civilians about their grievances, offering to hear Kony's side rather than condemn it. She pushed amnesty for defectors, arranged traditional cleansing ceremonies so ex-fighters could come home. And the listening worked where firepower hadn't: Kony — who took to calling her *"Mummy"* — agreed to meet, entered peace talks, declared himself willing to end the conflict. (The talks ultimately collapsed, friend — but not because listening failed. The government short-circuited it with a seven-day surrender ultimatum, and the war reignited. Uganda named her Woman of the Year for how close she came.)

The research backs what Bigombe practiced. Studies by Guy Itzchakov and Avraham Kluger show that people who talk to attentive, *nonjudgmental* listeners become less anxious, less defensive, explore their own views more deeply — and walk away with **more complex, less extreme attitudes**. They rethink *on their own*. Grant's name for the quality is lovely: **inverse charisma** — instead of dazzling people with what you say, you draw them out by how you listen. The best way into a closed mind, friend, is often through its owner's own voice.

## Before you go

So: Harish beat four hundred million articles by agreeing first, cutting his case to its strongest bones, and dancing instead of pulling; Rackham's experts plan for common ground, dilute nothing, and ask double the questions; the vaccine whisperer moved immovable mothers with twenty minutes of respect; and a warlord came to the table for the one person who listened. Fewer, better reasons. More, better questions.

But everything tonight was one person facing another, friend. Tomorrow the problem gets *bigger* and *older*: what happens when the disagreement isn't between two people but between two **tribes** — rivalries and hatreds passed down like heirlooms, where people despise a group they've never met because of a jersey, a border, a name? Grant walks into the oldest feud in American sports to find out whether tribal hate can be *unlearned*. Next time: **bad blood on the diamond.**

## Key takeaways

- A debate is a dance, not a war: open with common ground, concede valid points — credibility makes your remaining arguments hit harder.
- Argument dilution: every weak reason weakens your best one — cut your case to its one or two strongest bones.
- Rackham's experts spend a third of prep on common ground and ask more than double the questions of average negotiators.
- Never counterpunch into a defend–attack spiral — get curious, and narrate the process ("help me understand").
- Motivational interviewing: open questions, reflective listening, affirmed autonomy — people are persuaded by reasons they voice themselves.
- One respectful conversation moved vaccination rates 9 points; listening got a warlord to peace talks — inverse charisma is real influence.
