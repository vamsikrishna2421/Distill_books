# Bad Blood on the Diamond: Rethinking Us vs. Them

Sixth night, friend — and it starts with a confession that should worry you a little.

In surveys, hardcore Yankees and Red Sox fans admit — out loud, to researchers — that they would refuse small kindnesses to a rival fan. Some go further: they'd pass up free money if it meant a rival fan lost out too. Over a *baseball team*. And neuroscientist Mina Cikara found it goes deeper than trash talk: when die-hard fans watch their rival fail — even against some neutral third team — the **reward circuitry in their brains lights up**. Pleasure. Actual pleasure at strangers' suffering, and the more the circuit fires, the more willing they are to heckle, insult, and deny help in real life.

Tonight's question, friend: where does hatred like that come from — and can it be *unlearned*? The answer runs from a ballpark... to the most astonishing friendship in America.

## The accident of hatred

![A vast stadium at night split down the middle into two seas of fans in opposing red and navy colors, glaring across the aisle](/story-art/think-again/06-rivals.jpg)

Here's the first crack in the wall. Researcher Gavin Kilduff asked where rivalries actually come from, and found three mundane ingredients: **proximity** (the groups are near each other), **repeated competition** (they meet again and again), and **evenly matched records** (close contests raise the stakes). Read that list twice, friend — *nothing in it involves the other side being bad.* Rivalry is manufactured by geography and scheduling. Which means the moral contempt bolted onto it was never earned by anything the rivals did.

This is **social identity** at work. We define ourselves partly through groups — and clarity about who we are comes cheapest from clarity about who we're *not*. And once the wall is up, stereotypes seal themselves in three coats: we form beliefs about rival groups *without knowing their members* (the caricature fills the vacuum); we talk mostly to our own side, and **group polarization** makes every conversation a fresh dose of confirmation; and when a disconfirming case appears — a genuinely kind Yankees fan — we **subtype** them: "one of the good ones," an exception filed away so the rule survives untouched. That's why showcasing admirable members of the disliked group barely works. Grant tested it with baseball fans: reflecting on rival fans' positive qualities did almost nothing. The counterexamples were quarantined on arrival.

## The jogger in the wrong shirt

So what *does* widen the circle? A field experiment by Mark Levine with Manchester United supporters found the lever — and it isn't about the stranger at all.

::: guess
A jogger (secretly a researcher) slips and falls in front of Manchester United fans. In a Manchester United shirt, most stop to help; in a rival Liverpool shirt, only about a third do. Then the researchers changed ONE thing about the setup — not the jogger, not the shirt — and helping for the Liverpool runner doubled. What did they change?
---
**The question the fans had answered minutes earlier.** Before the "accident," researchers asked them to write about what they loved about being *football fans* — the broader identity, the game itself, rather than their club. After that one reframe, roughly **70 percent** stopped to help the Liverpool-shirted runner — double the rate. The stranger hadn't changed; the shirt hadn't changed. **The category had.** Whom we help depends less on their group, friend, than on how widely we've drawn the circle of our own. And Grant's own experiments with Yankees and Red Sox fans found the deepest version of the lever: **counterfactual thinking**. Ask a fan to seriously consider — *what if you'd been born in the other city? raised by a family that worshipped the other team?* Nearly everyone concedes they'd be cheering for the enemy... which quietly concedes that their allegiance reflects *birthplace, not virtue* — and the rival's reflects no vice. Fans who sat with that question became measurably less hostile: less willing to insult or sabotage, more willing to help. It works because it never attacks the belief head-on — there's nothing to defend; the person generates the destabilizing insight *themselves*. One warning from the same research: uniting rivals against a *common enemy* is a false shortcut — it doesn't reduce the prejudice, it just relocates it, and the us-versus-them habit gets stronger.
:::

## The pianist and the Klansmen

Now the maximum-difficulty test. **Daryl Davis** is a Black blues and boogie-woogie musician. One night after a set, a white audience member — astonished that a Black pianist could play like Jerry Lee Lewis — bought him a drink... and admitted he was in the Ku Klux Klan. Most people walk away from that conversation. Davis leaned in, with one question that became his life's method: *"How can you hate me when you don't even know me?"*

::: guess
For decades since, Davis has deliberately sought out Klansmen — including Imperial Wizards, the organization's leadership. His method involves NO debating, no arguing, no demands that they change. What does he do instead — and what have roughly 200 of them done?
---
He **listens**. At length, without interrupting; he asks genuine questions; he lets each man sit face-to-face with the contradiction between the stereotype in his head and the human being across the table. He doesn't set out to convert — he sets out to *understand* — and the rethinking happens on the other side of the table, at the other person's pace. It's the counterfactual experiment made flesh, friend: contact plus curiosity, destabilizing a category the believer thought was solid. The results: roughly **200 Klan members have renounced the Klan** through relationships with Davis — many pressing their robes and hoods into his hands, which he keeps as a record. Among them: **Roger Kelly**, an Imperial Wizard of the Maryland Klan, who rose *within* the organization even as the friendship deepened — then quit... and later asked Davis to be **his daughter's godfather**. Even identities built entirely on hatred can be rethought — and the lever was never a superior argument. But Grant flags an essential caveat, and hold it firmly: Davis *chose* this work. Nothing about his success obligates the targets of prejudice to sit with their haters and patiently educate them — that would stack a second injustice on the first. **The primary responsibility belongs to those who hold the prejudice — and to their in-group peers**, the messengers a bigot can hear without defensiveness. The white friend challenging the racist joke reaches places no outsider can.
:::

## Before you go

So: the brain takes real pleasure in a rival's pain — but the rivalry itself was manufactured from proximity, schedules, and close scores; stereotypes survive by quarantining every exception; one broader question doubled help for a fallen rival; the counterfactual — *what if I'd been born there?* — dissolves contempt at the root; and a bluesman collected two hundred robes armed with nothing but curiosity. Prejudice isn't defeated by proving the other group is good, friend. It's defeated by noticing the wall was built by accident — and that you'd be standing on the other side of it had the accident gone differently.

But every rethink so far has been one mind, one table, one conversation. Tomorrow the stakes multiply: how do you get a *whole room* to think again — a classroom of students certain of the textbook, a public gridlocked on its most charged, radioactive topics? There's a teacher who makes children rewrite the textbook itself, and a discovery about why everything we're shown about hot-button issues is designed to stop us thinking. Next time: **rethinking at scale.**

## Key takeaways

- Rivalry has three mundane ingredients — proximity, repeated competition, close records — none of which is the other side's fault.
- Schadenfreude is real: rivals' failures light up reward circuitry, and the activation predicts actual hostile behavior.
- Stereotypes self-seal: no contact, echo-chamber polarization, and "one of the good ones" subtyping quarantines every counterexample.
- Broaden the circle: fans primed as "football fans" helped a rival-shirted stranger at double the rate — categories drive kindness.
- The counterfactual dissolves contempt: "if I'd been born there, I'd wear their colors" — allegiance is birthplace, not virtue.
- Daryl Davis: curiosity and listening retired ~200 Klan robes — but the labor of dialogue belongs to bigots and their peers, never their targets.
