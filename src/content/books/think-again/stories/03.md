# The Joy of Being Wrong

Third night, friend — and it begins with one of the darkest experiments in the history of psychology.

Harvard, 1959. Psychologist Henry Murray invites undergraduates to spend *weeks* writing out their personal philosophies of life — their deepest beliefs, carefully argued — for what they're told will be a friendly debate with a fellow student. Then comes the ambush: each student is confronted instead by a law student *coached to deliver a brutal, personally insulting demolition* of everything they wrote. It's filmed. Then each student is made to watch the film of their own humiliation.

Most found it distressing. But the reactions varied wildly — and in that variance hides tonight's entire lesson.

## The inner dictator

![A shadowy throne room inside a glowing outline of a human head, a small dictator figure shredding incoming letters before they reach the throne](/story-art/think-again/03-dictator.jpg)

Some students hated every second. Others — strange as it sounds — found the demolition of their worldviews *stimulating*, even enjoyable. And one participant, code-named "Lawful," described the experience with rage that never faded. His name was **Ted Kaczynski** — later the Unabomber. Grant is careful: no one can know whether the study contributed to what Kaczynski became. But his manifesto reads as a case study in a mind that *cannot rethink* — total conviction, zero doubt, beliefs welded to identity with no seam left to open.

Why does a challenged belief feel like a physical assault? Psychologist Anthony Greenwald's answer: the **totalitarian ego** — an inner dictator whose full-time job is blocking threatening information, spinning failures into successes, and editing memory so your beliefs always look right. Attack a core opinion, and the dictator activates the bodyguards — you lapse into the preacher defending sacred ground, or the prosecutor indicting the critic — anything but the scientist testing a hypothesis. And the dictator's protection racket has a steep price, friend: **every fact it blocks is a lesson unlearned; every error it hides is a mistake you'll repeat.** The students who *enjoyed* Murray's gauntlet weren't thicker-skinned. Their sense of self simply didn't depend on their philosophies surviving intact.

## The two detachments

That separation can be built deliberately. Grant prescribes two **detachments** — not apathy, but a loosening of the weld between your self-worth and your current positions.

**First: detach your present from your past self.** Treat earlier versions of you as people you've outgrown. If past-you believed things present-you finds embarrassing — that's not a stain, *that's proof of growth*. And run the diagnostic in reverse, friend: **if your opinions today are identical to a year ago, you haven't been learning — you've been coasting.** Write down what you believed five years ago about work, money, politics; where nothing has moved, that's where to aim this week's learning.

**Second: detach your opinions from your identity.** When "I believe X" hardens into "I *am* an X-believer," every piece of evidence against X becomes an attack on *you* — and the dictator mobilizes. Kept separate, an opinion is just a *tool*: a current best guess, swappable the moment a better one arrives. And to the social sneer — "flip-flopper!" — Grant flips the frame beautifully: refusing to change your mind when the evidence changes is the *real* integrity failure. Consistency with old statements is loyalty to your past self. **Consistency with current evidence is loyalty to the truth.**

## Values, not beliefs

So if opinions shouldn't anchor identity — what should? **Values**: curiosity, honesty, growth, generosity, the pursuit of knowledge. *Who you are should be a question of what you value, not what you currently believe.*

The distinction does real work, friend. A values-based identity makes revision *self-affirming*: if you prize learning, admitting error is you at your *best*. A belief-based identity converts every update into an identity crisis — which is why people defend positions years after the evidence has collapsed. And values travel across changes beliefs can't survive: you can abandon a political stance, a theory, a strategy, a career plan and remain recognizably *you*, because the through-line is how you think and what you care about. Grant adds a lighter dividend: people who can *laugh* at their own past wrongness — comedy, not tragedy — measure happier and less anxious. Humor about your errors is detachment in action. The goal was never to enjoy failing at things that matter. It's to make "I was wrong" feel less like a confession... and more like a **status report** — the natural exhaust of an active mind running experiments.

## Updating as a competitive sport

Does the detachment actually pay? Return one last time to Tetlock's forecasting tournaments — because Grant draws from the same well, and finds the sharpest number in it.

::: guess
Among thousands of forecasters, the elite weren't distinguished by IQ, math skill, or expertise. One habit predicted accuracy above everything — and it can be counted. What was it, and what were the counts?
---
**How often they updated their beliefs.** Superforecasters revised roughly **four times per question**; ordinary forecasters, about **twice**. The strongest predictor of seeing the future was willingness to re-see it. Watch it embodied in Jean-Pierre Beugoms, military historian and superforecaster: in fall 2015, when pundits gave Donald Trump about a **6%** shot at the Republican nomination, Beugoms read the polling and media dynamics and concluded Trump was the *clear favorite* — a forecast that contradicted his own preferences, which is exactly the point. His discipline: ask *what would change my mind* before events force the question, and guard hardest against **desirability bias** — the pull to predict what you *want*. His colleague Kjirste Morrell — an MIT engineer with no political expertise — names the engine outright: she actively *enjoys* discovering she was wrong, "because it means I am now less wrong than before." For these people, friend, opinions are hypotheses on probation, and every update is a small *win* — a recalibration toward reality. Run the loop — humility, doubt, curiosity, update — more times than your rivals, and you beat them, whatever your starting expertise.
:::

## Kahneman's delight

One scene seals the chapter. Grant once presented research to Daniel Kahneman — the Nobel laureate who spent a lifetime cataloguing human bias — findings that flatly *contradicted* conclusions Kahneman had published.

::: guess
The world's foremost expert on motivated reasoning watches his own published conclusions get overturned in front of him. How did he react?
---
**His eyes lit up with genuine glee.** He was *thrilled* — and explained: *"Being wrong is the only way I feel sure I've learned anything."* His collaborators report he drops refuted ideas at a speed that unsettles them — because the ideas were never limbs of his identity; they were tools that stopped working. The moment of refutation isn't a loss, friend. **It's the moment his map snaps closer to the territory.** Two reframes make this joy available to anyone. First: being wrong isn't proof you're stupid — it's proof you were *paying attention when reality disagreed*. You were going to be wrong either way; the only choice is whether you find out. Second: prefer early, cheap corrections to late, expensive ones — a jab of discomfort today when a colleague punctures your plan beats a public failure next year when the market does. And the endpoint was never perpetual self-doubt: it's an identity built on being a *good learner* rather than a *right answerer* — an identity being wrong can never threaten, because **every discovered error is the identity doing its job.** This week: when someone proves you wrong, say so out loud, name what you learned — and notice that the sting is just the feeling of becoming less wrong.
:::

## Before you go

So: the inner dictator shreds the mail that could teach you; two detachments — from your past self and from your opinions — disarm him; values make a home that beliefs can move through; superforecasters win by updating twice as often; and the wisest man in the field greets his own refutation with delight. "I was wrong" — a status report, not a confession.

## Key takeaways

- The totalitarian ego blocks threatening facts and edits memory — its protection costs you every lesson it censors.
- Detach present from past self: identical opinions a year apart means coasting, not consistency.
- Detach opinions from identity: an opinion is a tool, and updating on evidence is loyalty to truth, not flip-flopping.
- Anchor identity in values (curiosity, integrity, growth) so changing your mind counts as living them.
- Updating won the tournaments: ~4 revisions per question versus 2 — and Beugoms forecast against his own wishes.
- Kahneman's glee: being wrong is how you know you learned — prefer the cheap early jab to the expensive late failure.
