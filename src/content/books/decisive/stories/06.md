# Attain Distance: 10/10/10 and Core Priorities

Sixth night, friend — and it opens in 1985, in an office at Intel, where two brilliant men have been stuck for months.

Intel is bleeding money in memory chips — the business it was *founded* on, the core of its identity. Japanese competitors are undercutting it relentlessly. The microprocessor line is thriving, but leadership can't bring itself to abandon "memories" — because abandoning memories feels like abandoning *Intel itself*. Every fact needed for the decision has been on the table for months. And the decision won't come.

Then president Andy Grove asks CEO Gordon Moore one strange question — and the deadlock breaks in *seconds*.

::: guess
Grove's question contained no new data, no analysis, no forecast. What did he ask Moore — and what did they do next?
---
**"If the board fired us and brought in a new CEO... what would that person do?"** Moore answered instantly: *get out of memories.* And Grove replied: **"Then why don't we walk out the door, come back in — and do it ourselves?"** Intel exited memory chips, bet everything on microprocessors, and became one of history's great growth stories. Notice what the question did, friend: Grove and Moore had possessed all the facts for months. What blocked them was **short-term emotion** — attachment to history, dread of admitting defeat, internal politics. A hypothetical outsider carried none of that baggage, *so the right answer became obvious in seconds*. That's the third villain and the third step of WRAP: **attain distance before deciding**. "Sleep on it" points the right way but is too weak — some feelings fade overnight (the crush on the showroom convertible), while others (anxiety, familiarity) persist for *years* and quietly steer you wrong. You need tools that manufacture distance **on demand**.
:::

## The three-horizon telescope

![Two executives in 1980s shirtsleeves standing at the threshold of a corporate office doorway, one gesturing back into the room, morning light through venetian blinds](/story-art/decisive/06-intel.jpg)

Tool one is Suzy Welch's **10/10/10**: audit the decision on three timelines — *how will I feel about this in ten minutes? ten months? ten years?* The Heaths tell of Annie, nine months into a relationship with cautious Karl, agonizing over whether to say "I love you" first. Ten minutes after speaking: nervous, but proud she took the risk. Ten months: almost certainly no regret at having tried. Ten years: whichever way he answered would scarcely matter. The analysis makes the choice nearly automatic — *speak up* — yet her in-the-moment fear had been treating the ten-minute horizon as *the only one that existed*. That's the tool's real function, friend: not to strip emotion out — your future feelings count too — but to **level the playing field**, forcing the vivid present emotion to share the stage with your calmer future ones. And it works in both directions: it deflates hot impulses (the purchase, the furious email)... and it *emboldens* you against short-term pain — the awkward conversation, the risky ask — by showing how fast the discomfort fades against the long-term payoff.

Why does the familiar feel so right? Two deep biases conspire, friend. The **mere exposure effect** — Zajonc showed that simply *encountering* something repeatedly (nonsense words, faces, logos) makes people like it more; familiarity alone, with no other merit, breeds preference. And **loss aversion** — losses loom roughly *twice to four times* larger than equivalent gains: offered a coin flip where tails costs $100, most people demand about $200 on heads before playing. Stack them and you get **status quo bias** — the gravitational force that kept Intel in memories, and keeps people in stale jobs and relationships. Both operate as *short-term emotions*: the comfort of the familiar and the sting of loss are felt **now**, while the benefits of change arrive later and abstractly. Which is why the fastest distancing tool in the book is one sentence, friend: **"What would I tell my best friend to do?"** The hopelessly tangled can usually answer *instantly* — and the answer is usually right — because advising a friend spotlights the single most important factor and ignores their fear of embarrassment entirely. Physicians use it on themselves: *what would I advise if this patient were my mother?* The perspective you'd give away for free is usually the best one you own.

## Who is your customer?

Sometimes distance isn't enough — because the difficulty is *genuine*: two things you truly care about are in conflict.

::: guess
Interplast — a nonprofit flying volunteer surgeons to repair cleft palates and burn scars in poor countries — was torn by endless board fights: Can volunteers bring spouses and kids? Can residents operate when a senior surgeon is safer? Should we even keep flying Americans in, or train local surgeons instead? One question settled ALL of them at once. What was it?
---
**"Who is our customer — the volunteer, or the patient?"** They chose **the patient** — and that single declaration resolved the entire cluster: residents' training mattered less than patient safety; empowering local surgeons served patients best *even though* it made American volunteers less central. Some offended volunteers left... and the organization became dramatically easier to run, friend, because **a thousand future fights had been settled in advance.** That's the general law: when a decision keeps you agonizing for days, it's usually a clash between **core priorities** — and *naming the winning priority* converts an emotional tug-of-war into a solvable question, "unweighting" countless later decisions. But declaring priorities is cheap, friend — the scarce resource is *time*, and lesser obligations devour it by default: research on managers found roughly **90 percent squander their time on busyness** — only one in ten spends it purposefully. Core priorities lose not to villains but to *inboxes*. Two countermeasures: Jim Collins's **stop-doing list** — born of a mentor's thought experiment: *you inherit $20 million... but have ten years to live — what do you stop doing right now?* — maintained as seriously as any to-do list, because making room for a priority mathematically requires *evicting* something. And **defend calendar time**: Collins tracks his hours against explicit targets like a budget. Every incursion on the protected block is what it is, friend: *a small decision to betray a big commitment.*
:::

## Before you go

So: Grove's revolving door — fire yourself, rehire yourself, and do what the outsider would; 10/10/10 makes the loud ten-minute feeling share the stage with the ten-year one; mere exposure plus loss aversion equals the gravity of the status quo; the best-friend question strips a decision to its essentials in one sentence; and when agony persists, it's a priority conflict — name the customer, keep the stop-doing list, guard the calendar. This week, friend: take the decision you've chewed longest, and answer — *what would you tell your best friend to do?* Then take your own advice.

One working chapter remains before the finale, friend — and it faces the last villain head-on: your certainty about the future. Tomorrow: why you should stop betting on one forecast and *bookend* tomorrow instead — planning simultaneously for failure and for wild success; the safety margins that absorb what you didn't see coming; and why software teams and Hollywood studios both learned to assume they're wrong. ## Key takeaways

- Grove's new-CEO question: distance deletes ownership and dread — walk out the door, come back in, and do the obvious.
- 10/10/10: let ten minutes, ten months, and ten years all vote — the present feeling gets an outsized ballot by default.
- Mere exposure + loss aversion = status quo bias: the familiar is felt now, the gains of change arrive later and abstractly.
- The best-friend question strips decisions to the single most important factor, immune to your short-term fears.
- Agonizing decisions are priority conflicts: Interplast's "the patient is the customer" settled a thousand fights in advance.
- Priorities need enforcement: a stop-doing list and protected calendar blocks — 90% of managers lose their time to busyness.
