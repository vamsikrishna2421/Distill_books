# Reality-Test: Consider the Opposite

Fourth night, friend — and it opens in the candlelit halls of the Vatican, in the year 1587.

Pope Sixtus V has just created the strangest job in Christendom. Whenever the Church considers making someone a saint, one canon lawyer is appointed to stand up — *against* the saint. His formal title is "promoter of the faith," but the world knows him as **the devil's advocate**: his official, sanctioned, holy duty is to argue that the miracle evidence is thin, that the candidate's character had flaws, that the halo is premature. He isn't a troublemaker, friend. **He's doing his job** — and the job existed because the Church understood something about human nature four centuries before the psychologists measured it:

When we favor a conclusion, we don't *evaluate* evidence. **We recruit it.** Tonight: the second villain — confirmation bias — and the machinery that beats it.

## Your mind argues like a lawyer

![A lone canon lawyer in dark robes gesturing forcefully before a tribunal of seated cardinals in a candlelit Renaissance Vatican hall, dust motes in the shafts of light](/story-art/decisive/04-advocate.jpg)

The scale is measured, friend: a meta-analysis of 91 studies and more than 8,000 people found we are **over twice as likely** to seek confirming information as challenging information — and the skew *worsens* exactly where it matters: charged topics, and positions we've already invested identity in. Psychologist Tom Gilovich exposed the trick in the questions we silently ask ourselves: for conclusions we *like*, we ask **"CAN I believe this?"** — and one supportive scrap grants permission. For conclusions we *don't* like: **"MUST I believe this?"** — and one flaw grants dismissal. Same evidence, opposite verdicts, depending only on which standard of proof we quietly applied. And here's why intelligence doesn't save you, friend: smart people don't escape the bias — **they build more sophisticated one-sided briefs.** More analysis inside a rigged search just stacks agreeable data higher. The fix is structural.

::: guess
For four centuries the devil's advocate scrutinized every sainthood. Then in 1983, Pope John Paul II effectively abolished the office. What happened to canonizations?
---
**They exploded — John Paul II named more saints than his predecessors of the preceding several centuries COMBINED.** Remove the institutional skeptic, friend, and the approval rate tells you exactly what happened to scrutiny. So build the skeptic in: General Motors' legendary chairman Alfred Sloan, facing a committee's *quick unanimous agreement* on something important, would propose adjourning — taking the matter up next meeting, so disagreement had time to *develop*. For Sloan, instant consensus wasn't a green light; **it was evidence nobody had reality-tested the idea yet.** The military's version is the **murder board**: a panel whose explicit assignment is to kill the plan in a conference room — because better there than in the field. Two design notes make engineered dissent work, friend: *protect the dissenter* — criticism must be a sanctioned role, not a career risk — and you don't need heavy machinery: a rotating devil's-advocate hat, or simply giving your existing skeptics cover to argue, captures most of the benefit.
:::

## Questions that could hurt you

The second tool aims your own questions at your assumptions' soft spots. **Disconfirming questions** presume problems exist and ask what they are.

::: guess
Researchers ran a role-play: participants sold a used iPod that had twice frozen and wiped all its music — a defect buyers would badly want to know. Disclosure depended almost entirely on how the buyer PHRASED one question. Three versions: "Tell me about it," "It doesn't have any problems, does it?", and "What problems does it have?" What were the disclosure rates?
---
**8 percent... 61 percent... 89 percent.** The polite opener got the defect volunteered *one time in twelve*; the disconfirming question — *"what problems does it have?"* — extracted it nine times in ten. Sellers rarely lied outright, friend — they simply let comfortable questions go unpunished. The disconfirming buyer signaled that evasion wouldn't work. Use it wherever information is asymmetric and interests diverge: the used car, the business acquisition, the reference check — *"why did the last person leave?"*, never *"everything's fine, right?"* One honest caveat: aggressive probing fits *adversarial* settings. With people facing a power gradient — employees, patients — hard leading questions intimidate rather than reveal; there, open prompts draw more truth. The constant is the intent, friend: **ask the question whose answer could genuinely change your mind — not the question that flatters your plan.** And borrow the diagnostician's ritual against the anchor of the first plausible answer: good doctors, guarding against "premature closure," run trained questions against their own conclusions — *What else could this be? What about this case doesn't fit? Could two things be going on at once? What's the worst it could be — and have I ruled that out?* The first explanation is a candidate, never a verdict. Make it **earn** the confidence you were about to hand it for free.
:::

## What would have to be true?

Sometimes the problem isn't too little disagreement but too much of the wrong kind — camps dug in, each running its own confirmation-biased hunt for ammunition. Young consultant Roger Martin faced exactly that in a hostile strategy fight over a failing copper mine. Asking the room *what they believed* would have ignited a war of assertions. Instead he changed the question: for each option on the table — **"what would have to be true for this to be the best choice?"** The reframe is quietly brilliant, friend: nobody concedes anything, so the belief-protection machinery never engages. Advocates must spell out the *conditions* under which their option wins — prices, costs, competitor moves — converting vague conviction into a testable checklist; skeptics articulate what evidence *would* convert them, instead of sniping. Then the group goes and *checks* — and the analysis, not the loudest voice, eliminates options. **It converts advocates into analysts and arguments into research plans.** The same move works on your private verdicts about people: PepsiCo's Indra Nooyi called it the best advice she ever received — *assume positive intent*. When someone's behavior stings, deliberately test the charitable reading. It often fits the facts just as well — and responding to it goes better *even when you were partly right to be annoyed*.

## Before you go

So: your mind is a defense attorney — twice as hungry for confirmation, sliding between "can I" and "must I" as convenience requires; the Church licensed a skeptic for four centuries, and sainthood exploded the year he was fired; Sloan tabled unanimous decisions, murder boards kill plans indoors; "what problems does it have?" beats politeness eleven-to-one; and when debate deadlocks, ask what would have to be true. This week, friend: spend fifteen minutes hunting *only* for evidence that your current favorite plan is wrong — and write down the strongest case against it.

But even honestly gathered evidence, friend, still gets read through a distorting lens: the view from *inside* your own special case. ## Key takeaways

- Confirmation bias recruits evidence: 2-to-1 skew toward confirming data, worse on charged and identity-laden questions.
- Gilovich's tell: "Can I believe this?" for what you like, "Must I believe this?" for what you don't — same evidence, rigged verdicts.
- Engineer dissent: devil's advocates, murder boards, and Sloan's rule that instant unanimity means nobody has tested the idea.
- Disconfirming questions extract truth: "What problems does it have?" got 89% disclosure versus 8% for the polite opener.
- The diagnostician's ritual: what else could this be, what doesn't fit, could two things be going on — first ideas are candidates, not verdicts.
- Deadlocked? Ask "what would have to be true" — it turns advocates into analysts; and with people, test positive intent first.
