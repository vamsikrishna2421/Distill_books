# Prepare to Be Wrong: Bookend the Future

Seventh night, friend — and it opens in a hospital, with the most sobering statistic in this whole book.

Researchers compared physicians' diagnoses of intensive-care patients against the ground truth later revealed by autopsy. Focus on the doctors who declared themselves **"completely certain"** of their diagnosis — the strongest possible confidence, from highly trained experts staring at rich clinical data. How often were the completely certain... *wrong*?

**Roughly forty percent of the time.** Sit with that, friend. If certainty tracks accuracy that poorly in medicine, what is your confidence about the product launch, the hire, the career move actually *worth*? The fourth villain is overconfidence — and tonight's answer isn't to predict better. Decades of research say experts are dreadful at point predictions. The answer is stranger and stronger: **stop needing the prediction to be right.**

## Bookend the future

![A businessman standing on a catwalk overlooking a vast warehouse filled to the ceiling with endless crates of white plastic pump dispensers, shafts of light from high windows](/story-art/decisive/07-softsoap.jpg)

The mistake isn't ignorance about the future — it's treating the future as a *point* when it's actually a **range**. We spotlight one scenario, usually the one we want, and plan as if it were guaranteed. The fix is to **bookend**: sketch a distinctly bad outcome on one end, a distinctly great one on the other, and *prepare for both*. Investor Byron Penstock never asked "what will this stock be worth?" — he estimated a plausible *floor* and *ceiling*, and bought only when the price sat near the floor: a wrong guess cost little, a right one paid handsomely.

The lower bookend's tool you've met on other shelves, friend — Gary Klein's **premortem**: announce that it's one year later and the project has *failed spectacularly*, then have everyone independently write the reasons. The tense is the trick: "what might go wrong?" invites polite hedging, but a fictional *fait accompli* unlocks what researchers call **prospective hindsight** — imagining an event as already-having-happened generates about **30% more correct reasons** than mere forecasting. And the premortem legitimizes dissent: the skeptic isn't disloyal — *they're playing the assigned game*.

::: guess
The upper bookend is the Heaths' "preparade": assume runaway triumph — are you READY for that? In the late 1970s, Robert Taylor of tiny Minnetonka Corp invented Softsoap — liquid soap in a pump bottle — knowing he held no meaningful patent, and that Procter & Gamble could copy it the instant it proved popular. He asked the preparade question: if this takes off, what will EVERYONE need? Then he bet nearly his company's entire net worth. On what?
---
**The pumps.** Only two manufacturers in America could make the plastic pump tops — so Taylor ordered **one hundred million of them**, roughly $12 million, locking up the nation's entire supply for an estimated 18 to 24 months. When Softsoap boomed, the giants *couldn't get pumps* to copy it at scale — and the head start entrenched a brand worth hundreds of millions. Taylor didn't *predict* success, friend — **he prepared for it.** The preparade converts vague optimism into operational questions: how do we hire fast enough? can suppliers scale? which bottleneck breaks first at ten-times demand? Run both exercises together and you've bookended the decision: the premortem hardens the plan against the bad tail, the preparade positions you to *exploit* the good one — and neither requires knowing which future will arrive. **Which is precisely the point, friend. You don't.**
:::

## Margin for the problems you can't name

Premortems catch *foreseeable* failures. For the rest, borrow the engineer's habit: the **safety factor** — deliberate excess capacity that absorbs unknown unknowns. Elevator cables, friend, are built to hold roughly **eleven times** their maximum rated load. The engineers don't know which stress or freak event the margin will someday absorb — they simply know *reality reliably outruns calculation*, so they buy insurance in steel. Veteran software managers do the same, padding every engineer's estimate — not because engineers lie, but because they're estimating *the version of the project where nothing goes wrong... a version that has never once shipped.* The transferable rule: **when a plan's numbers feel precise, treat the precision as the error.** And note the division of labor: a premortem fix is *targeted* — "the vendor might slip, line up a backup"; a safety factor is *generic* — "something we haven't imagined will bite, leave a month unallocated." Robust plans carry both. Eleven-fold cable strength looks paranoid, friend... right up until you remember how rarely elevators kill anyone.

::: guess
The last tool handles a subtler future: not catastrophe, but predictable disappointment. John Wanous studied "realistic job previews" — employers candidly telling candidates a job's WORST features before they accept: the angry callers, the grinding travel, the tedium. Turnover reliably dropped. But the mechanism wasn't what you'd guess — few candidates actually walked away. So why did honesty work?
---
**Vaccination.** A small, controlled dose of the negative reality, administered *in advance*, lets people pre-process the pain, rehearse coping — and, crucially, interpret the eventual unpleasantness as **normal** rather than as evidence they made a terrible mistake. Honesty also breeds trust, friend: people cut you slack when they feel they were told the truth up front. And the logic generalizes everywhere: warn the new customer about the learning curve; tell the family moving cities about the lonely first six months; brief the first-time manager on how bruising performance conversations feel. In each case the negative event is *close to certain* — the only choice is whether people meet it **inoculated or blindsided**. Together the toolkit is complete: premortem and safety factors guard the bad futures, the preparade readies the great one, and realistic previews inoculate everyone against the ordinary disappointments guaranteed to come. None of it requires forecasting skill — because as the Heaths insist, *the point of preparing to be wrong is that you will be.* **Humility about prediction, paired with concrete preparation, beats confident planning across almost any future that actually shows up.**
:::

## Before you go

So: the completely certain are wrong forty percent of the time, and the future is a range, not a point; bookend it — premortem the failure, preparade the triumph, and buy the pumps before the world knows it needs them; pad for the unnameable like an elevator engineer; and vaccinate everyone, including yourself, with the truth about the guaranteed disappointments. This week, friend: take your current confident plan and write both bookends — the realistic worst case and the runaway best — then check the plan survives one and can exploit the other.

One night remains in this book — and it answers the question all this preparation raises: once you've decided and launched, *how do you know when to wake up?* Tomorrow, the finale: the tripwires that snap you out of autopilot; the deadline David Lee Roth wrote into a rock band's contract using a bowl of brown M&Ms; and the quiet, freeing truth the whole book has been building toward — that decisiveness comes from trusting the *process*, not from feeling certain. Next time: **set tripwires — and trust the process.**

## Key takeaways

- "Completely certain" physicians were wrong ~40% of the time — confidence is a feeling, not a forecast.
- The future is a range: bookend it — plan for a distinctly bad outcome AND a distinctly great one, like Penstock's floor and ceiling.
- Premortem the failure ("it's a year later; it failed — why?"): prospective hindsight yields ~30% more correct reasons than forecasting.
- Preparade the success: Taylor bought America's entire pump supply because he asked what runaway triumph would require.
- Safety factors absorb the unnameable — elevator cables at 11x, padded schedules — precision in a plan is usually the error.
- Realistic previews vaccinate: an advance dose of the worst features cuts turnover and turns disappointment into "normal," not "mistake."
