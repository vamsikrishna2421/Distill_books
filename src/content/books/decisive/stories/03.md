# Multitrack — and Find Someone Who's Solved Your Problem

Third night, friend — and it opens on a bus, rattling six hundred miles across the American Midwest in the 1950s.

The passenger is a small-town shopkeeper from Arkansas. He's heard a rumor: some Ben Franklin variety stores up in Minnesota are trying something strange — **one centralized checkout line** at the front, instead of a register at every counter. Six hundred miles, by bus, to look at *cash registers*. He watches. It works. He copies it. The shopkeeper's name is **Sam Walton**, the copied idea becomes a Walmart standard, and years later he'd say it plainly: *"most everything I've done I've copied from somebody else."*

Hold that bus ticket, friend — it's the cheapest R&D in history, and it's tonight's second great tool. The first: how to run more than one option at a time *without* doubling the work. (And yes — last night I promised you a fruit company in crisis. Forgive an old storyteller: it wasn't bananas. It was *berries*. You'll see.)

## Two ads at once beat one at a time

![A man in a 1950s suit and hat gazing thoughtfully out the window of a rattling intercity bus, small-town storefronts and grain fields sliding past in morning light](/story-art/decisive/03-walton.jpg)

::: guess
Stanford researcher Steven Dow gave designers a real task — web banner ads — with the same total time budget. One group worked SERIALLY: design one ad, get feedback, revise, repeat. The other worked in PARALLEL: several ads at once, feedback on all, then converge. Who won — and what happened to their egos?
---
**The parallel group won on every measure** — higher click-through rates in a live campaign, better expert ratings, more creative diversity. Comparing options taught them the *landscape* of the problem — what makes an ad work at all — while the serial designers merely polished a single premise. And the psychological bonus is the quiet masterpiece, friend: serial designers, fused to their one design, **took criticism personally and improved less**; parallel designers heard feedback as information about *the designs*, not verdicts on *themselves*. **When you have only one option, your ego is bolted to it.** Nor does speed suffer — it *improves*: Kathleen Eisenhardt studied Silicon Valley executive teams and found leaders weighing several alternatives simultaneously actually decided **faster** than the one-at-a-time grinders — comparison builds understanding quicker, alternatives prevent escalating commitment to a failing favorite, and a scouted fallback means setbacks don't trigger panicked restarts. Two guardrails, friend: options must be **real** — a *sham option* exists only to flatter the favorite (the realtor's two dumps before "the one"; Kissinger's menu of nuclear war, current policy, or surrender). The test: *would you feel a genuine pang if this option were forbidden?* And don't fear paralysis — the famous jam study froze shoppers at **24** jams, not at two or three. The bar is beautifully low: *from one option to two or three.*
:::

One more dimension of widening, friend: options carry a *mindset*. **Promotion focus** plays to win — chasing gains; **prevention focus** plays not to lose — guarding what you have. Under stress, people and companies snap into pure prevention, and the option set collapses into flavors of "hunker down."

::: guess
Ranjay Gulati studied ~4,700 companies through three global recessions. Only about 9% emerged truly flourishing. They weren't the hardest cost-cutters, and they weren't the boldest spenders. What did the winners do — and what were their odds of becoming post-recession leaders?
---
**They did both at once** — cutting operational fat *selectively* while simultaneously investing in R&D, marketing, and assets positioned for the recovery. That blended promotion-AND-prevention group had a **37% chance of breaking away as post-recession leaders** — far better odds than firms locked in either pure mindset. The lesson scales straight down to a single life, friend: laid off, most people ask only prevention questions — *how do I pay the bills, how do I find the same job fast?* The promotion lens adds the missing options: *is this the moment to switch fields, start the thing, move cities?* Neither lens alone. **Every important decision deserves at least one "protect what we have" play and one "pursue the opportunity" play** — and when they seem to conflict, remember the winners didn't choose between defense and offense. *They ran them in parallel.*
:::

## Someone has already solved it

When you can't generate options, friend — stop inventing and start *hunting*, in three places. **Inside — find the bright spots**: Kaiser Permanente's Alan Whippy discovered sepsis was implicated in roughly *one in four* hospital deaths yet got a fraction of the attention — and rather than inventing a response, Kaiser *spread practices already proven* by its own pioneers: routine screening, lactate measurement, code-alert urgency for early signs. Rolled across 21 hospitals, sepsis mortality dropped well below the national average. If one region's sales team thrives, or you once quit smoking for three days — *interrogate the bright spot.* **Outside — study competitors**: that's Walton's bus. Scouting isn't cheating, friend; it's option generation at someone else's expense. And when nobody has solved your *exact* problem — **ladder up**: someone solved a version of it. Scientist-watcher Kevin Dunbar found analogy was the workhorse of real lab breakthroughs — "what is this problem *like*?" And here come the berries: Lexicon Branding, hired to name a new PDA, deliberately *refused to stare at the product* — literal staring yields generic names. The insight: PDAs make people feel stressed and rushed. So the team hunted things that *slow people down and evoke pleasure*... which led to berries... and to **BlackBerry** — whose tiny keys even resemble the fruit's drupelets. The rule of the ladder, friend: low rungs give usable-but-ordinary options; high rungs give original-but-risky ones. *Climb only as high as you need.* And for decisions you make repeatedly, keep a **playlist** — a pre-built menu of proven approaches (a cost-cutting playlist with a dozen levers beyond layoffs) — so future decisions start wide *by default*.

## Before you go

So: parallel beats serial on quality, speed, *and* ego; sham options decorate foregone conclusions — demand the pang test; blend promotion with prevention like the 9% who flourished; and before inventing anything, hunt — bright spots inside, Walton's bus outside, the analogy ladder above, and a playlist for next time. This week, friend: take your biggest pending decision and force one real Plan B beside it — even a sketch changes how you see Plan A.

But options, however wide, still have to survive the second villain, friend: the evidence-gatherer inside you who's secretly building a case. ## Key takeaways

- Multitrack: parallel options beat serial on quality and speed — and feedback lands on the designs, not on you.
- Sham options decorate foregone conclusions — real options pass the pang test: you'd mind losing them.
- Paralysis is a disease of two dozen options, not two or three — the bar is just one more real alternative.
- Blend mindsets: one protect-what-we-have play plus one pursue-the-gain play — Gulati's 9% ran defense and offense in parallel.
- Someone solved your problem: bright spots inside (Kaiser's sepsis), competitors outside (Walton's bus), analogies above (BlackBerry).
- Keep playlists for recurring decisions — bottle past option-generation so you start wide by default.
