# The Munger Decision Framework: Checklists, Opportunity Cost, Two-Track Analysis

Seventh night, friend — and it begins in a cockpit at dusk.

Watch the captain before takeoff. Thirty years of experience, ten thousand hours in the air — and still she pulls out the laminated card and reads every line aloud. Flaps. Trim. Fuel. Every flight, every time. Why? Because aviation learned, in blood, that experienced professionals left to memory *skip steps* — and the skipped step is never the one that matters... until the day it kills you.

Charlie built his entire decision-making life on that lesson. Tonight, the machine assembles: everything from six nights — the latticework, the inversion, the twenty-five tendencies — snaps together into a working system you can actually run.

The engine is **two-track analysis**. Track one: *what are the factors that really govern this situation, rationally considered?* — the cold work of math, economics, engineering, biology. Track two, and here's the genius, friend: *what subconscious tendencies are at work — in the other people, and IN ME?* Is he defending this position merely because he already owns it? Trusting that projection because someone was paid to produce it? Track two runs *defensively* — which of the twenty-five are fooling me right now? — and *offensively* — how are they driving the customers, the competitors, the market? (Remember: the Coca-Cola pitch was track-two thinking used *constructively*.) And Charlie's startling claim: a person of moderate intelligence running *both* tracks routinely will beat a brilliant person running only the first. Rationality first, because psychology explains nothing about whether a bridge will hold — but the psychological pass is where the catastrophic errors hide: a track-one analysis can be technically flawless and still be the product of a mind quietly bent by a fee, a crowd, or a prior public commitment.

And the delivery system is the pilot's: **checklists**. Knowing a hundred models is useless if you retrieve the two vivid ones — that's the man-with-a-hammer reborn as a memory failure. So march through the list, deliberately, every time. Checklists defeat *omission* — the biggest errors are questions never asked. They force you across disciplines. They resist the premature closure that doubt-avoidance and stress push you toward. And run them *in full*, friend — finding one problem is no proof there isn't a second... and when several items all point the same *favorable* way, that convergence is the rare flag of a lollapalooza opportunity.

::: guess
At Berkshire Hathaway, a shiny new acquisition proposal arrives. Here's the question that decides its fate in minutes: what does that new idea have to BEAT? Not the S&P 500. Not the market average. Something much harder. What?
---
**Buying more of what Berkshire already owns and understands.** That's **opportunity cost** — Charlie's master filter, friend. Every use of money, time, or attention is measured against *your own best available alternative* — never against averages, benchmarks, or what the crowd is buying. If the new idea isn't *clearly* better than adding to your best existing holding, the answer is no — regardless of how attractive it looks in isolation. *Nothing is judged in isolation.* And watch what this one filter generates: **concentration** — rank your opportunities honestly and a tiny handful dominate, so diversifying beyond them means deliberately buying your *twentieth-best idea*. **Ruthless speed** — most proposals visibly fail the standing alternative within minutes, and no further analysis is owed them. And a **self-raising hurdle** — every good decision makes your best alternative better, which raises the bar for the next one. That quiet ratchet, compounding over decades, is the engine behind Berkshire's whole record. It governs your hours too, friend: time spent analyzing a mediocre, complicated situation is time not spent waiting, alert and ready, for a great simple one.
:::

## The three trays

![An airline cockpit at dusk, a pilot's hand resting on a laminated checklist card, glowing instrument panels and a violet-orange horizon through the windshield](/story-art/poor-charlies-almanack/07-cockpit.jpg)

On Charlie's mental desk sit three trays: **in**, **out**, and **too hard** — and the third tray, friend, is by far the fullest. This is the **circle of competence**: each of us can genuinely understand only a limited set of businesses and problems, and returns come from staying strictly inside. The crucial skill isn't *enlarging* the circle — lifelong learning does that slowly — it's knowing *exactly where the edge is*. A modest circle with a well-marked boundary beats a grand circle with a fuzzy one.

The sorting is fast and guiltless. Most technology transitions? Too hard. Macroeconomic predictions? Too hard. Turnarounds needing five things to go right in a row? Too hard. Charlie treats *"I don't know"* as a fully respectable, capital-preserving answer — in direct defiance of the doubt-avoidance and overoptimism tendencies that demand a verdict on everything. And in the "no" tray, disqualifiers run *first*: dishonest management, a commoditizing business, any table where you'd be the patsy. One clear disqualifier ends the analysis, friend — **there is no partial credit for an idea that's 90 percent attractive and 10 percent fatal.**

::: guess
What survives into the "yes" tray is astonishingly rare — and that rarity demands a strange temperament. Warren Buffett has a famous image for it: imagine you were handed a card at the start of your investing life, and that card set a lifetime limit. A limit on what?
---
**Twenty decisions.** A punch card with **twenty slots** — one punched for every investment you ever make, and when the card is full, you're done forever. Live under that card, friend, and everything changes: every decision would be researched exhaustively, sized to *matter*, and made only when the odds were overwhelming. That's the temperament the whole framework demands — **extreme patience married to extreme decisiveness**. Patience first: genuine "yes" opportunities arrive a handful of times per decade, so most of the investing life is spent reading, learning, holding cash, resisting the itch — Charlie sides with Pascal: *all of humanity's problems stem from man's inability to sit quietly in a room alone.* But patience without its twin is worthless: when the fat pitch finally crosses the plate — typically amid panic, when Mr. Market is manic-depressive and every tendency in the catalog screams *stand back* — **bet heavily**. A few big, correctly-sized, patient decisions account for essentially all of Charlie's wealth. And the hinge between the two traits, friend, is **preparation**: the person who acts instantly in a crisis did the analysis *years* earlier and was only waiting for the price. Opportunity meets the prepared mind.
:::

## The ten commandments of the method

The book compresses the whole practice into ten principles, run *simultaneously*, friend — a checklist for the checklist-maker: **Risk** — margin of safety always, and never deal with people of questionable character. **Independence** — you're right because your facts and reasoning are right, not because others agree. **Preparation** — go to bed a little wiser than you woke. **Humility** — stay in the circle, hunt disconfirming evidence, and never forget you're the easiest person to fool. **Rigor** — value isn't price, progress isn't activity, wealth isn't size. **Allocation** — capital's best use is the top job; nothing you own is sacred at the right price. **Patience** — compound interest is the eighth wonder; never interrupt it unnecessarily. **Decisiveness** — be fearful when others are greedy, greedy when others are fearful. **Change** — recognize when the world has genuinely changed, and be willing to kill your best-loved ideas. **Focus** — keep it simple; reputation is losable in an instant; face big trouble directly, never sweep it under the rug.

## Before you go

So: run every serious decision on two tracks — the rational factors, then the audit of your own wiring; deliver your models by checklist, like the captain at dusk; measure everything against your best alternative, not the average; keep three trays and let "too hard" stay the fullest; and pair the patience to sit quietly for years with the ferocity to punch a card slot when the fat pitch comes. This week, friend: for one big pending commitment, name the *specific alternative it must beat* — and write down which two tendencies are most likely bending your judgment about it.

One night remains in this book. And it's not about money at all. Tomorrow, the question underneath all seven nights: why did Charlie insist that the highest compliment isn't "brilliant" but *trusted* — and what did he mean by wanting his life wrapped in a **seamless web of deserved trust**? The last night is about character, friend — Franklin's heir, the lost eye, the fortune given away, and the epitaph worth working for. Next time, the finale: **deserved trust.**

## Key takeaways

- Two-track analysis: solve it rationally with the latticework, then audit both yourself and the other actors for the 25 tendencies — moderate intelligence on both tracks beats brilliance on one.
- Fly by checklist: the biggest errors are questions never asked; run the full list, and treat convergent favorable signals as the lollapalooza flag.
- Opportunity cost is the master filter: every new idea must beat buying more of your best current holding — and every good decision raises the hurdle.
- Three trays — in, out, too hard — and too hard stays fullest; a well-marked boundary beats a big fuzzy circle, and "I don't know" is a respectable answer.
- Disqualifiers first: no partial credit for 90% attractive, 10% fatal.
- Temperament over IQ: sit quietly for years (Pascal), then bet heavily on the fat pitch (the 20-slot punch card) — preparation is what links the two.
