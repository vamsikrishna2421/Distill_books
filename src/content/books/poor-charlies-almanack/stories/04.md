# Turning $2 Million into $2 Trillion: The Coca-Cola Thought Experiment

Tonight, friend, Charlie stops preaching and performs. This is the most dazzling fifteen minutes in the whole book — a magic trick done with freshman-level ideas.

Picture it: Atlanta, 1884. You stand before a rich man named Glotz with an outrageous proposition. He puts up two million dollars for half of a new business. The conditions: it must forever be called Coca-Cola, it must sell a non-alcoholic drink, and — here's the impossible part — it must be worth **two TRILLION dollars** a hundred and fifty years later, in 2034... *while paying out fat dividends the whole way*. You have fifteen minutes to convince him.

A million-fold multiplication, friend. It sounds like lunacy. Charlie solves it on stage — in 1996, to an audience of scholars — using five simple notions: settle the **no-brainer questions** first; get numerically fluent like **Fermi**, who estimated the atomic bomb's power from scraps of falling paper; think **forward AND backward**; run a **multidisciplinary checklist**, not one department's toolkit; and remember that giant effects come from **combinations** — tuberculosis was only tamed, friend, by three drugs given *together*. Extreme outcomes demand engineered confluences, not single causes.

First no-brainer: no ordinary beverage operation multiplies money a million-fold. Only a business built on a **legally protected trademark** — a name that can own a corner of every human mind on earth — has a chance. Second: it must charm *everyone* — Atlanta first, then America, then the world. So the pitch is really about building a universal brand. But that raises the real question...

::: guess
Charlie says that to reach $2 trillion, you must understand what this company is ACTUALLY manufacturing. Not a drink — the syrup is almost beside the point. Billions of dollars of advertising, ships full of concentrate, coolers on every street on earth... all in service of manufacturing WHAT?
---
**Conditioned reflexes.** In billions of human nervous systems, friend. The trademark is the *stimulus*; purchase and ingestion are the *response* — and the whole business is a reflex-manufacturing machine built from three psychology engines running at once. **Operant conditioning**: every serving must deliver *real* rewards — sugar's food value, caffeine's kick, flavor and aroma engineered as strong stimuli, icy relief in hot climates — and the product must be available *everywhere, always*, because if a thirsty person can never conveniently reach a rival, the rival can never retrain the reflex. **Pavlovian conditioning**: mere *association* — the exotic name, and relentless advertising tying the drink to whatever people already love: happiness, sports, celebration, beautiful lives. That ad spending isn't a cost to minimize, friend — *it IS the engine*, and the biggest seller can afford the most conditioning per serving, which feeds itself. **Social proof**: people drink what they see others drinking, so visible ubiquity — every hand, every screen, every shelf — is free advertising that actually improves the *perceived* taste. Three forces, mutually reinforcing, scaled to the planet: a deliberately engineered **lollapalooza**. And the operations follow from the psychology like footnotes: ship concentrated syrup, not water — water is everywhere and shipping it is waste; serve it cold; and keep the flavor *novel* rather than imitating cherry or vanilla — a new taste is harder to copy, easier to protect, impossible to compare away.
:::

## The arithmetic of an impossible promise

![A glass of dark cola with beads of condensation on a polished wooden bar counter, golden late-afternoon light streaming through a window, soft warm bokeh behind](/story-art/poor-charlies-almanack/04-colaglass.jpg)

Now watch Fermi's ghost do the math, friend — because two trillion stops being scary the moment you break it into sips.

Every human being must drink about **sixty-four ounces of water a day**. Suppose flavored, enjoyable beverages can plausibly capture a quarter of that intake worldwide — and suppose our company wins *half* of that new-drink market by 2034. With world population heading toward eight billion, that's about **2.92 trillion eight-ounce servings a year**. Net just **four cents** of profit per serving and you're earning roughly **$117 billion a year** — enough, with growth still ahead, to be worth two trillion dollars even after decades of generous dividends.

"But four cents? When the drink must start out cheap?" Here Charlie reaches *outside* psychology for an assist: **inflation**. Over 150 years the dollar will predictably lose the great bulk of its purchasing power — assume something like 95 percent, friend — so four future cents is a fraction of one 1884 penny. Meanwhile real incomes keep rising, and people will happily pay a trivial price for a cheap, reliable pleasure. Decomposed into per-serving arithmetic, the heroic goal becomes... *ordinary*. That's what numerical fluency is FOR.

::: guess
Then Charlie flips the board — invert, always invert. To be worth $2 trillion, there are exactly FOUR things this company must NEVER do. And here's the delicious part: the REAL Coca-Cola Company — the actual stewards of the actual franchise — went on to commit or nearly commit TWO of them. What are the four never-dos?
---
**One: never let the drink develop a satiating, cloying aftertaste.** Biology says organisms stop consuming when the body signals "enough" — so the flavor must be engineered, by trial and error, to leave *no lingering aftertaste*, so a heavy user can drink can after can on a hot day. Food science in service of evolution's rulebook. **Two: never lose the trademark — not even half of it.** If "cola" drifts into a generic word anyone can use, the conditioned value leaks out of the vault; defend the name with permanent legal ferocity. **Three: never provoke envy.** A franchise this dominant invites resentment, so stay cheap, harmless, honestly valuable — conduct that stirs envy summons the political attacks that kill giants. **Four: once your flavor owns the world, NEVER change it abruptly.** The conditioned reflex plus deprival-superreaction means a taste change registers in loyal brains as *theft of something they own* — even if the new formula wins blind taste tests. And history graded the exam, friend: the real company signed **perpetual bottling contracts at fixed syrup prices** — nearly ruinous once a century of inflation arrived, a blunder elementary forward-thinking forbids. And in **1985 it launched New Coke** — replaced the sacred flavor, detonated exactly the furious backlash the backward analysis predicts, and retreated within months. The owners of the greatest psychological franchise on earth *did not understand the psychology that built it.*
:::

## The indictment

And that, friend, is where Charlie's cheerful magic trick turns into a courtroom. He has just solved an "impossible" problem using nothing beyond first-year ideas — a little biology, a little psychology, a little arithmetic, elementary economics and trademark law. His verdict: virtually no business school, psychology department, or law school in America could produce this answer. Not because the knowledge is missing — *every needed idea sits in introductory textbooks* — but because nobody teaches **assembly**. Psychology departments run clean one-variable experiments and rarely study how tendencies *combine* in messy reality — yet the combinations are where all the power is. Business schools teach marketing without the underlying mechanics. Economics models rational agents who could never explain New Coke. Each silo defends its turf... and the cross-boundary problems — which is to say, *most real problems* — belong to no one. The highest-value intellectual skill, Charlie says, is the one most systematically untaught.

## Before you go

So: settle the no-brainers first; Fermi-check every big goal — customers times frequency times unit profit — until "impossible" becomes ordinary arithmetic; know what you're *really* manufacturing (usually a habit, a reflex, a feeling — not the object); engineer combinations, because lollapaloozas beat single causes; and always run the four-never-dos exercise — plan backward for the mistakes that would kill you, because even Coca-Cola's own stewards didn't. This week, friend: take one product or habit you're building and audit its reward engine — what real reward does each use deliver, what associations surround it, and where can people *see others using it*?

Now — Charlie has spent four nights mentioning these psychological tendencies like a man patting a locked trunk. Tomorrow he opens it. His masterwork: the complete catalog of the twenty-five standard ways the human mind fools itself — the very machinery that built Coca-Cola and detonated New Coke. We take the first fourteen, friend, starting with the most powerful force he knows: *never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about incentives.* Next time: **the psychology of human misjudgment, part one.**

## Key takeaways

- Five notions crack giant problems: no-brainers first, Fermi arithmetic, forward-and-backward thinking, a multidisciplinary checklist, and engineered lollapalooza combinations.
- Coca-Cola's real product is conditioned reflexes: operant rewards in every serving, Pavlovian association through advertising (the ad spend IS the engine), and social proof through visible ubiquity.
- Fermi math tames "impossible": 64 oz/day × ¼ flavored × half the market × 8 billion people ≈ 2.92T servings; at 4¢ each that's ~$117B/year — and inflation makes the 4¢ ordinary.
- Invert for the four never-dos: no satiating aftertaste, never lose the trademark, never provoke envy, never abruptly change a beloved flavor.
- History's proof: fixed-price perpetual bottling contracts and New Coke — the franchise's own stewards didn't understand the psychology that built it.
- Every needed idea is in freshman textbooks; what's missing is assembly — synthesis across silos is the highest-value skill nobody teaches.
