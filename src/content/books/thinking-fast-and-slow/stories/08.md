# Risky Choices: Rare Events, Mental Accounts, and Frames

Eighth night, friend — and it opens with a wager between two economists at lunch.

Paul Samuelson — a Nobel laureate, no less — offered a colleague a simple coin flip: heads, you win $200; tails, you lose $100. A wonderful bet by any arithmetic. The colleague *refused*... and then added something revealing: "But I'll happily take **a hundred** of those flips."

Feel the contradiction, friend? A bundle of a hundred attractive bets is built entirely out of bets he rejects *one at a time*. The hundred flips together are worth about $5,000 in expectation with a vanishingly small chance of losing anything — and by refusing each flip individually, he'd forfeit all of it. That's **narrow framing**: evaluating every risk in its own little box, where loss aversion — last night's 2:1 monster — vetoes each one. The cure is **broad framing**: see each gamble as one draw from a lifetime portfolio of similar draws, and adopt Kahneman's mantra for tolerable risks: *you win a few, you lose a few.* (Conditions apply, friend: the gambles must be independent, no single loss can threaten your wealth, and no desperate long shots.) Better still, replace case-by-case emotion with a standing **risk policy**: always take the highest insurance deductible you can afford; *never* buy the extended warranty; check your portfolio quarterly, not daily — every peek at a dip is a fresh dose of loss-pain, and it triggers churn. Companies catch the disease too: ten division managers, each loss-averse in their own box, collectively strangle a portfolio of projects their CEO would love.

::: guess
Seymour Epstein's lab offered people a prize for drawing a red marble, choosing between two urns. Urn A: 10 marbles, 1 red — a 10% chance. Urn B: 100 marbles, 8 red — an 8% chance. The arithmetic is trivial and printed right there. Which urn did people prefer?
---
**Urn B — the worse one.** A full third to nearly half of subjects chose 8-in-100 over 1-in-10, friend, because System 1 doesn't compute ratios — it *pictures marbles*. Eight winning marbles beat one winning marble in the imagination, and the denominator quietly vanishes: **denominator neglect**. It's why "a vaccine that harms *1 child in 100,000*" terrifies parents more than "a 0.001% risk" — the first frame conjures one damaged child; the second is bloodless arithmetic. And it's half the story of how we handle **rare events**: when a risk is *vividly described*, we wildly overweight it — Kahneman caught himself avoiding Israeli buses during the bombing years, though the per-ride risk was tiny; as Sunstein showed, once a vivid threat grabs attention, worry barely budges between 1-in-1,000 and 1-in-a-*million*: **probability neglect**. But there's a boundary with a nasty bite, friend: when we learn probabilities only from *experience* — sampling life day by day instead of reading the odds — rare events get **underweighted**, because most days contain no earthquake. That's the complacency before every flood, quake, and financial crash: the person who has never seen the disaster acts as if its probability were zero. Either way — panic or complacency — the images and feelings drive, and the numbers ride in the back seat.
:::

## The secret ledgers

![Two glass urns filled with marbles on a laboratory table, the near one small with a single red marble glowing among white ones, the larger one crowded with marbles, dramatic side light](/story-art/thinking-fast-and-slow/08-urns.jpg)

Richard Thaler's great discovery, friend: money is fungible, but *we* are not. We keep **mental accounts** — a vacation fund, a gas budget, a private ledger for every stock — and we crave closing each little account *in the black*. The bookkeeping is emotional, not financial.

Watch what it does. Odean's brokerage data showed investors overwhelmingly **sell their winners and keep their losers** — because selling a winner closes an account with a profit (a small parade), while selling a loser stamps FAILURE on the ledger (a small funeral). It's exactly backwards: taxes reward realizing *losses*, and recent winners tended to keep outperforming. The purchase price, friend, is a ghost — it has no bearing on what the stock will do next; only future prospects matter. Scale the same error up and you get the **sunk-cost fallacy**: the firm that has poured $50 million into a doomed project keeps pouring, because closing the account at a loss is unbearable — better to let the losses land on the *next* CEO's ledger. Kahneman's homely version: two fans plan to drive through a blizzard to a game — one paid for his ticket, one got it free. The money is gone either way, the blizzard is identical... and the *payer* is far more determined to risk the drive. Sometimes the kindest fix for an organization, friend, is a new manager — one with *no ledger to defend*.

And hovering over every ledger is **regret** — with a cruel asymmetry: losses born of *action* sting far more than identical losses born of *inaction*. Mr. Paul considered switching stocks and didn't; Mr. George switched. Both missed the same $1,200. Everyone agrees George suffers more — he *acted*. That asymmetry makes us pay absurd premiums for peace of mind, cling to defaults, order defensive tests, buy the blue chip nobody was ever fired for buying. Kahneman's counsel: price regret *explicitly* before deciding, remember your **psychological immune system** — regret fades far faster than you predict — and never compound a loss with hindsight's lie that the risk was obvious all along.

::: guess
Two neighboring countries, similar cultures, similar values. In Austria, nearly 100% of citizens are organ donors. In Germany next door... about 12%. No sermon, no ad campaign, no cultural chasm explains it. What does?
---
**A checkbox, friend.** In Austria, donation is the *default* — you must opt OUT. In Germany, you must opt IN. Neither box is hard to tick; almost nobody ticks either one — and so a stroke of form design decides, quite literally, who lives waiting for a kidney. That's the power of **frames**: logically identical choices, opposite outcomes. The most chilling demonstration used doctors: told a lung-cancer treatment has **"90% one-month survival,"** 84 percent of physicians recommended surgery; told it has **"10% one-month mortality"** — *the same fact* — only half did. Medical training provided no immunity. In the famous Asian disease problem, "save 200 of 600 for certain" makes people risk-averse, while "400 of 600 die for certain" — identical — makes them gamblers; De Martino's brain scans show the *amygdala* lighting up as people follow the frame. Merchants know: a credit-card *surcharge* enrages, a *cash discount* soothes — same pennies. Even fuel economy lies in its frame, friend: upgrading a 12-mpg truck to 14 saves MORE fuel than upgrading a 30-mpg car to 40 — invisible in miles-per-gallon, obvious in gallons-per-1,000-miles. Some frame is unavoidable — so the honest move is to *choose the frame that serves the decision*... and to deeply distrust any preference of yours that dissolves when the words are rearranged.
:::

## Before you go

So: rare risks are overweighted when vivid and underweighted when merely lived-past — write the probability down in one format and see if your fear tracks it; take the hundred-flips test on every small favorable gamble and adopt standing risk policies; audit your ledgers — sell by future prospects, not purchase price, and let no sunk cost buy another dollar; price regret before acting and trust it to fade; and reframe every big choice at least twice — survival as mortality, cost as loss, default as active choice — before you let it stand. This week, friend: check your investments *once*, not daily... and decline one extended warranty with a smile.

One night remains, friend — the last chapter of the last book on the library shelf. And Kahneman saves his most haunting discovery for the end: there are **two of you** — one who lives your life, and one who remembers it — *and they disagree about what makes it good*. Why did patients prefer a LONGER colonoscopy? Why would you replay a two-week vacation but photograph it to death? Tomorrow, the finale: **the two selves.**

## Key takeaways

- Narrow framing is expensive: Samuelson's colleague refused one great flip but wanted 100 — ask "would I take a hundred of these?" and adopt standing risk policies (max deductibles, no extended warranties, quarterly portfolio checks).
- Denominator neglect: 8-in-100 beats 1-in-10 in the imagination — vivid rare risks get overweighted, while experienced-but-unseen ones (quakes, crashes) get underweighted toward zero.
- Mental accounts drive the disposition effect (sell winners, keep losers — backwards for taxes and momentum) and sunk costs (the ticket-payer drives into the blizzard); purchase price is a ghost.
- Regret is stronger for action than inaction — priced into vaccines, defensive medicine, and blue chips; price it explicitly, and remember it fades faster than predicted.
- Frames are not wrappers: 90% survival vs 10% mortality flipped physicians 84%→50%; an opt-out checkbox makes Austria ~100% organ donors vs Germany's 12%.
- Reframe twice before deciding — and distrust any preference that dissolves when the problem is reworded.
