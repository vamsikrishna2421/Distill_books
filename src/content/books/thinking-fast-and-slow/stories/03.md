# Jumping to Conclusions: WYSIATI and Substitution

Third night, friend — and it opens in a laboratory where people are shown a human face for **one tenth of a second**. A camera-flash of a face. Then researcher Alex Todorov asks one question: *which of these two people looks more competent?*

Silly parlor game? The faces were real candidates in U.S. Senate races. And those tenth-of-a-second snap ratings — driven by nothing but a strong chin and a confident half-smile — predicted the actual winners of about **seventy percent** of Senate, House, and governor's races. It gets worse, friend: the effect was roughly *three times stronger* among politically uninformed voters who watch a lot of television. The less real information the slow mind has... the more the face rules the vote.

Tonight is about that machinery: how System 1 leaps to conclusions, seals them, and never files a report. Start small: *"Ann approached the bank."* You pictured marble columns and tellers — unless the previous sentence mentioned a river. Your mind resolved the ambiguity instantly, picked ONE meaning, and *never told you an alternative existed*. And believing works the same way, friend — psychologist Daniel Gilbert showed that merely *understanding* a statement begins with *believing* it; UN-believing is a second, effortful operation that needs System 2. Load people's memory with digits while they read nonsense claims, and later they remember the false claims as *true* — the un-believer was busy. That's why persuasive ads run late at night, when the guard has gone to bed.

::: guess
Solomon Asch showed people a description of a man named Alan: intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious. Then a man named Ben: envious, stubborn, critical, impulsive, industrious, intelligent. People liked Alan FAR more than Ben. Read the two lists again, friend. What's the trick?
---
**They're the same six words — reversed.** The first traits set the frame, and everything after is *bent to fit*: stubbornness in an intelligent man reads as *principled conviction*; intelligence in an envious man reads as *dangerous cunning*. That's the **halo effect** — one known trait colors every unknown one, because System 1 wants a single coherent story and gaps don't stop it. Kahneman caught it red-handed *in his own grading*, friend: reading each student's exam booklet straight through, a brilliant first essay lifted his marks on every essay after. When he switched — grading all answers to question one across every booklet, then question two — his scores lost their tidy coherence, he felt *less* confident... and was *more accurate*. The discomfort was the sound of the halo breaking. The general cure is one of the most useful rules in this book: **decorrelate error.** Independent judgments carry errors that partially *cancel*; judgments made after discussion share the same errors and merely swell the confidence. One loud early guess ruins the wisdom of the crowd. So the meeting rule, friend — steal it tomorrow: **before any issue is discussed, every person writes their position down.** Otherwise the room becomes a chorus behind whoever spoke first and loudest.
:::

## What you see is all there is

![A courtroom witness stand dramatically lit from one side by a shaft of light, the other half of the wood-paneled room falling into deep darkness, empty jury benches in shadow](/story-art/thinking-fast-and-slow/03-courtroom.jpg)

Now the chapter's crown jewel, friend — the clumsy acronym Kahneman chose *because* it's clumsy, so you'd remember it: **WYSIATI. What You See Is All There Is.**

System 1 works only with the ideas currently lit up. Information it doesn't have — never presented, never retrieved — *might as well not exist*. It builds the best story from what's on the table, and here's the treacherous part: **your confidence is set by the story's coherence, not the evidence's quality or quantity.** A tale built on flimsy, one-sided evidence often feels *more* compelling — fewer facts, fewer contradictions.

The proof is brutal. Subjects heard evidence from a real legal dispute: some got only the plaintiff's side, some only the defense, some both. The one-sided listeners *knew* they were hearing half the case... and were **more confident** in their verdicts than the people who heard everything. Coherence beat completeness, friend — while wearing a sign saying "I'm incomplete."

WYSIATI is the mother of biases we'll meet all week: *overconfidence* — we never allow for the evidence we don't have; *framing* — "90% survival" and "10% mortality" are the same fact, but System 1 never builds the other frame on its own; *base-rate neglect* — the vivid description is *seen*, the statistic isn't. And in the background, the machine computes constantly whether you ask or not: it judges *averages* instantly but is blind to *sums*; it matches intensities across any dimensions — crimes to punishments, a girl's reading precocity to a man's height; and its **mental shotgun** fires extra assessments you never ordered — people are slower to reject a false statement *when it rhymes*.

::: guess
German students were asked two questions: "How happy are you these days?" and "How many dates did you have last month?" In that order, the answers were UNRELATED — correlation essentially zero. Then researchers made one tiny change... and the correlation leapt to 0.66, one of the strongest in survey research. What changed?
---
**They swapped the order.** Dates first, happiness second — and suddenly dating *became* happiness, friend. The dates question stirred up a romance-tinged feeling, and when the big vague question "how is your life?" arrived a moment later, System 1 simply *handed over the feeling it already had*. That's **substitution** — Kahneman's master key to all heuristics: when a question is hard, the fast mind quietly swaps in an *easier* one, answers that, and never mentions the switch. "How much would you give to save endangered dolphins?" becomes "How much do I *feel* when I picture dying dolphins?" "How popular will the president be in six months?" becomes "How popular is he *now*?" The answer arrives with full confidence — and lazy System 2 signs it. The most consequential version is Paul Slovic's **affect heuristic**: your *likes decide your beliefs*. In the real world, a technology's risks and benefits are largely independent facts; in human heads they're locked in opposition — what we like feels low-risk and high-benefit, what we dislike feels the reverse. Slovic's killer result: reading a persuasive case for a technology's *benefits* lowered people's estimates of its *risks* — though not one word about risk was provided. The feeling moved first; the "facts" fell in line behind it. In those moments, friend, System 2 isn't a critic. **It's a lawyer.**
:::

## Before you go

So: a tenth of a second of face decides elections among the uninformed; understanding begins with believing, and tired minds can't un-believe; the same six words make a hero or a menace depending on order — break halos by judging dimensions separately and collecting opinions *before* discussion; confidence measures the story's coherence, never the evidence's completeness — what you see is all there is; and when a hard question gets an instant answer, the question was secretly swapped. This week, friend: in your next meeting, get every position in writing before anyone speaks. And when you answer something hard *instantly*, stop and name the easier question you probably answered instead — it's usually "do I like this?"

## Key takeaways

- System 1 resolves ambiguity silently and believes by default — un-believing needs System 2, so tired and distracted minds are gullible (hence late-night ads).
- The halo effect bends later evidence to fit first impressions (Alan vs Ben — same words, reversed); grade dimensions separately, like Kahneman's question-at-a-time exams.
- Decorrelate error: independent judgments cancel errors, discussion amplifies them — collect written positions before any group debate.
- WYSIATI: confidence tracks story coherence, not evidence quality — one-sided listeners were more confident than those who heard both sides.
- A tenth-of-a-second face judgment predicted ~70% of Senate races — basic assessments run constantly and leak into votes, hires, and trust.
- Substitution is the master heuristic: hard questions get swapped for easy felt ones (dates→happiness at r=0.66); the affect heuristic makes likes decide beliefs, with System 2 as the lawyer.
