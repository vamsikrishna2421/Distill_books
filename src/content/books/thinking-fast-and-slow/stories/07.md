# Prospect Theory: Losses Loom Larger

Seventh night, friend — the summit. Tonight is the idea that won the Nobel Prize, and it starts with two millionaires and a puzzle so simple a child could solve it... which three hundred years of economics could not.

Meet Jack and Jill. Today, each of them has exactly **five million dollars**. According to the economics that ruled from Daniel Bernoulli in 1738 until 1979, they should be equally happy — same wealth, same utility, same smile. But yesterday, friend, Jack had *one* million... and Jill had *nine*. Jack is dancing on the table. Jill is staring into her coffee. Identical wealth — opposite worlds. Because human beings don't experience *states* of wealth. We experience **changes** — gains and losses from a **reference point**, the place we're standing when the news arrives.

How did brilliant economists miss this for three centuries? Kahneman calls it **theory-induced blindness**: once a theory becomes your working tool, its flaws become nearly invisible — every anomaly gets explained away instead of counted. The fix he published with Amos Tversky in 1979 — **prospect theory** — rests on three principles. **Reference dependence**: the same lukewarm bowl feels hot to the hand that came from ice water and cold to the hand that came from hot. **Diminishing sensitivity**: the felt difference between $900 and $1,000 is tiny next to the difference between $100 and $200. And the third, friend — the crown jewel — deserves its own question.

::: guess
I offer you a coin flip. Tails: you pay me $100. Heads: I pay you... how much? Feel your own answer before you tap, friend — what's the SMALLEST heads-payoff that would make you take this bet?
---
If you're like most people, you wanted around **$200** — twice the potential loss — before the flip felt worth it, friend. And there it is: **loss aversion**, the most consequential number in behavioral science. Across hundreds of measurements the ratio lands between 1.5 and 2.5 — call it **two to one**: *losses loom larger than gains.* The value curve is kinked at the reference point, steeper on the downside — and evolution explains why: creatures that treated threats as more urgent than opportunities left more descendants. From this one asymmetry, whole landscapes of behavior unfold. We're **risk averse for gains** — take the sure $900 over a 90% shot at $1,000 — but **risk seeking for losses** — prefer a 90% chance of losing $1,000 to a certain $900 loss, because a *sure* loss is unbearable. And ownership itself moves the reference point: Richard Thaler noticed a professor who'd never pay more than $35 for a bottle of wine, yet *refused to sell* his own bottles for $100. At Cornell, students given coffee mugs demanded about **$7** to part with them while buyers offered **$3.50** — the same mug, doubled in value by the mere fact of *being yours*. That's the **endowment effect**: giving up codes as a loss, acquiring as a mere gain. (Its boundaries prove the mechanism, friend: shoe merchants feel no pang selling shoes — goods held *for exchange* don't count; seasoned traders show no effect; and the desperately poor show none either, for the grimmest reason — living below their reference point, *every* choice is a choice among losses.) It even poisons negotiation: my concession is *my loss and your gain*, so both sides always feel they gave more than they got — and potential losers of any reform fight roughly twice as hard as potential winners push. The status quo has a bodyguard, friend, and its name is loss aversion.
:::

## The fourfold pattern

![A professional golfer crouched low on a green at sunset, reading the line of a putt, ball and hole in sharp focus, long amber shadows across manicured grass](/story-art/thinking-fast-and-slow/07-putt.jpg)

Now add the second distortion, friend: probabilities don't enter our decisions at face value. Moving from *impossible to possible* — 0% to 5% — creates hope out of nothing, and we pay wildly for it: that's the **possibility effect**, and it sells every lottery ticket on earth. Moving from *nearly sure to sure* — 95% to 100% — kills the last worry, and we pay dearly for that too: the **certainty effect**. (Maurice Allais humiliated a room full of the world's best economists in 1952 with choice problems proving *they* violated their own axioms near certainty.) Kahneman and Tversky measured the warp precisely: a 1% chance *feels* like 5.5; a 90% chance feels like only 71.

Cross the warped probabilities with gains versus losses and you get the **fourfold pattern** — four rooms we all live in. *Likely gain*: fear of disappointment makes us cautious — plaintiffs with strong cases settle cheap. *Unlikely gain*: hope makes us gamblers — lotteries thrive. *Unlikely loss*: dread makes us buyers of insurance at any price. And the deadly fourth room, friend — *likely loss*: the sure loss is so intolerable that we become **reckless** — defendants with weak cases litigate to the bitter end, failing companies double down, and losing wars grind on for years, because every alternative to hope feels like amputation. Whoever negotiates against you repeatedly can farm these rooms like an orchard.

::: guess
Economists Pope and Schweitzer analyzed over 2.5 MILLION putts by professional golfers — same distances, same greens, same pros. They compared putts for BIRDIE (a gain: one under par) with identical putts to save PAR (avoiding a bogey — a loss). Elite athletes, maximum stakes, identical geometry. What did they find?
---
**Par putts dropped 3.6 percent more often than identical birdie putts, friend.** When the putt was to *avoid a loss* — a bogey — the pros focused harder, and it showed, across millions of strokes. Par is the **reference point**; a bogey is a *loss*, a birdie merely a *gain* — and even the greatest performers on earth, with millions on the line, could not switch the asymmetry off. Had they putted birdies the way they putted pars, a top player like Tiger Woods would have earned about **a million dollars more per season**. Goals become reference points, friend — anywhere, for anyone. New York cab drivers appear to set a *daily income target*: on busy rainy nights when every hour pays double, many *quit early* — target hit; on dead days they grind long hours chasing it. Exactly backwards from maximizing income... and exactly right once you see that stopping short of the target *feels like a loss*. Underneath it all hums **negativity dominance**: one angry face pops out of a happy crowd — a happy face never pops from an angry one; threats reach the amygdala before consciousness does; bad reputations form faster than good. And John Gottman's marriage research puts a number on the tax, friend: lasting couples need positive moments to outnumber negative ones by about **five to one**. Bad is stronger than good — so budget accordingly, in money and in love.
:::

## Key takeaways

- Bernoulli's 300-year error: we evaluate changes from a reference point, not states of wealth — Jack and Jill hold the same $5M in opposite emotional worlds.
- Loss aversion ≈ 2:1 — a $100-loss coin flip needs ~$200 upside; risk averse for gains, risk seeking for losses.
- The endowment effect doubles the price of what's yours (Cornell mugs $7 vs $3.50) — but only for goods held for use; traders and the desperate poor are immune for opposite reasons.
- Probabilities warp: possibility effect sells lotteries, certainty effect sells cheap settlements — 1% feels like 5.5%, 90% like 71%.
- The fourfold pattern's deadly room is likely loss: sure losses feel unbearable, so defendants litigate, failing firms double down, and losing wars drag on.
- Goals are reference points: pros sink par putts 3.6% more than identical birdies, cabbies quit early on rainy days — and bad is stronger than good everywhere (marriages need ~5:1).
