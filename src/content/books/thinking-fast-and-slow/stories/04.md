# The Heuristics: Anchors, Availability, and Linda

Fourth night, friend — and it opens with a carnival trick played on unsuspecting university students... by two of the greatest psychologists who ever lived.

Kahneman and Tversky wheeled a big **wheel of fortune** into the room — secretly rigged to stop only at **10** or **65**. Each student watched it spin, noted the number, then answered a factual question: *what percentage of United Nations members are African nations?* The wheel, mind you, was *obviously random* — everyone saw it spin. And yet: students whose wheel stopped at 10 guessed **25 percent** on average. Students whose wheel stopped at 65 guessed **45 percent**. A carnival wheel moved their beliefs about the world by twenty points, friend. That's **anchoring**: any number in the room before you estimate pulls your estimate toward it — invited or not.

It works two ways. Sometimes lazy System 2 starts at the anchor and adjusts... stopping too early — ask people whether Gandhi died before or after age *144* and their eventual guess floats absurdly high. And sometimes the anchor works as a *prime*: a high listing price makes your eye find the house's virtues; a low one, its flaws. The pull is measurable — the **anchoring index** runs about **55 percent** of whatever distance you shift the anchor. Told the tallest redwood is more or less than 180 feet, visitors guessed 282; told 1,200, they guessed 844. And professionals? Real-estate agents touring the *same house* with different fabricated list prices produced valuations 41 percent anchored — barely better than business students' 48 — *while insisting the list price hadn't touched them*. Even a supermarket sign reading **"limit of 12 per person"** doubled soup purchases from 3.3 cans to 7. The defenses all require waking System 2, friend: *name* the anchor out loud, deliberately argue the opposite... and when an opening offer is outrageous, refuse to counter inside its range at all. Walk, or reset the table.

::: guess
Now, before the tricks, a mystery worthy of a detective. The U.S. counties with the LOWEST kidney-cancer rates share a clear profile: rural, sparsely populated, in traditionally Republican states. Fresh air? Clean living? Careful, friend... because here's the twist: the counties with the HIGHEST kidney-cancer rates are ALSO rural, sparse, and Republican. What's going on?
---
**Sample size — nothing else.** Small populations swing to extremes in BOTH directions purely by chance, friend: a county of three thousand people needs only a handful of cases — or none — to top or bottom the national table. Rural life neither causes nor prevents kidney cancer; small samples simply *lie loudly*. This is the **law of small numbers** — our intuition that small samples resemble their population. They don't. The Gates Foundation and others spent roughly **$1.7 billion** promoting small schools after noticing small schools were overrepresented among top performers... never checking that they were *also* overrepresented among the worst, for the same statistical reason. Even professional research psychologists — Kahneman and Tversky's first joint discovery — routinely chose sample sizes too small to detect the very effects they were hunting. And the deeper disease is the one we met last night: System 1 suppresses doubt and spins causal stories from noise. Londoners in the Blitz saw deliberate targeting in bombing patterns that analysis proved random. The basketball **"hot hand"** turned out to be what chance alone produces. The rule, friend: when a small sample shows a dramatic pattern, your FIRST hypothesis is *luck* — and your first question is "how big was the sample?"
:::

## What comes to mind feels common

![A carnival wheel of fortune mid-spin, blurred with motion under strings of warm glowing bulbs at dusk, a wooden boardwalk stall in rich golden and red tones](/story-art/thinking-fast-and-slow/04-wheel.jpg)

The next heuristic hides in every argument you've ever had about chores, friend. Ask each spouse *"what percentage of the housework do you do?"* — and the answers reliably sum to **well over 100 percent**. Nobody's lying. Each person's own efforts simply *come to mind more easily* — you were there for all of yours. That's **availability**: asked "how frequent?", System 1 answers "how easily do examples surface?" — and ease tracks drama, recency, and personal experience as much as truth. Plane crashes feel likelier the week after one leads the news.

Norbert Schwarz found a delicious twist: the *feeling* of retrieval matters more than the content. People asked to recall **six** examples of their own assertiveness later rated themselves *more* assertive than people asked for **twelve** — because dredging up twelve is a struggle, and the struggle itself is read as evidence: *if it's this hard, I must not be assertive.* And Slovic's risk research shows where this leads at national scale: people judged accidents and disease equally deadly — though disease kills about **eighteen times** more; the 1989 **Alar** scare over an apple chemical of negligible risk devastated an industry through a runaway **availability cascade** — story feeds coverage, coverage feeds alarm, alarm feeds coverage. As Slovic puts it, friend: *whoever controls the definition of risk controls the policy.*

::: guess
And now the most famous woman in the history of psychology. Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student she was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice. Which is more probable, friend — (a) Linda is a bank teller, or (b) Linda is a bank teller AND active in the feminist movement?
---
Did something in you lean toward **(b)**? So did **85 to 90 percent** of university students — including graduate students in decision science — and it is *logically impossible*, friend. Every feminist bank teller IS a bank teller; a conjunction can never be more probable than either of its parts. But (b) tells a better *story* — it fits Linda — and System 1 sells plausibility as probability. The great Stephen Jay Gould confessed that even knowing the right answer, *"a little homunculus in my head continues to jump up and down, shouting at me."* That's the **conjunction fallacy**, and its lesson stings: **every detail you add to a scenario makes it more convincing and less likely.** (The fix is frequency framing: ask "of 100 women like Linda, how many are...?" and most people suddenly get it right.) The same prototype-over-sum blindness prices goods, friend: Christopher Hsee showed people dinnerware — Set A: 40 pieces, 9 broken; Set B: 24 pieces, all intact. A contains everything in B *plus extras*. Seen side by side, everyone paid more for A. Seen ALONE... people paid $33 for B and only **$23** for A — the broken pieces poisoned the average, and System 1 judges by averages, never sums. Less was more. So it is with forecasts: strip every prediction to its bare claim before you price its probability.
:::

## Before you go

So: small samples scream and lie — ask for the sample size before accepting any story; every number in the room pulls your estimate ~55 percent of the way, even when a carnival wheel supplies it — name the anchor and argue against it; easy recall masquerades as frequency, effort of recall masquerades as evidence, and cascades turn trace risks into panics; stereotypes trample base rates; and detail makes stories more believable while making them less true — Linda is a bank teller, friend, full stop. This week: before any negotiation, write down the first number you expect to hear and three reasons the truth sits far from it.

Tomorrow night, one of the most quietly mind-bending ideas in the whole book — the one Kahneman called his most satisfying *eureka*. Why did an Israeli flight instructor swear that *praise ruins pilots and screaming improves them* — and why was he wrong about everything except his own experience? Why do rookie stars slump in year two? It isn't pressure, friend. It's mathematics. Next time: **regression to the mean.**

## Key takeaways

- Law of small numbers: tiny samples hit extremes in both directions — kidney-cancer counties and the Gates Foundation's $1.7B small-schools mistake; ask "how big was the sample?" first.
- Anchoring pulls estimates ~55% of the anchor shift — even from a rigged carnival wheel; professionals deny it while showing it (realtors 41%); name the anchor, argue opposite, never counter inside an outrageous range.
- Availability: easy recall reads as frequency (housework shares sum >100%), and the struggle of recall reads as evidence (6 vs 12 assertiveness examples).
- Availability cascades inflate trace risks into panics (Alar 1989) — whoever controls the definition of risk controls the policy.
- Representativeness discards base rates for stereotypes (Tom W.); anchor on the base rate and ask how diagnostic your evidence really is — statistics teach nobody, instances do.
- Linda: a conjunction can never beat its parts — added detail raises believability and lowers probability; and System 1 values by averages, not sums (broken dishes cheapen the whole set).
