# Define the Real Problem, Then Widen Your Options

Fourth night, friend — and it opens in a conference room you've sat in a hundred times.

The meeting starts. Someone — usually the most senior or the fastest talker — says, *"The problem is we need more salespeople."* And watch what happens: every mind in the room *snaps to that frame*. Within ninety seconds, the whiteboard is filling with hiring plans, budgets, timelines. The energy is wonderful. The room feels productive.

Nobody notices, friend, that **"we need more salespeople" isn't a problem at all — it's a solution wearing a problem costume.** Nobody vetted the frame. Whoever spoke first defined it, and the whole meeting is now brilliantly, efficiently, expensively solving... nobody checked *what*. Tonight: the most expensive failure in decision-making — the elegant solution to the wrong problem — and how the clearest thinkers refuse to fall for it.

## A decision is not a choice

![A confident man at a whiteboard in a glass conference room commanding the discussion, colleagues nodding and taking notes, one skeptical woman at the table's end holding a pen still](/story-art/clear-thinking/04-warroom.jpg)

First, a sharp line, friend. A **choice** is picking an option — something you can do reflexively, with the four defaults doing the picking. A **decision** is a choice made with *conscious evaluation*. By this definition, most people make very few actual decisions in their lives — and if you reacted without thinking, you didn't decide badly; *you didn't decide at all*. The deliberate process has stages — define the problem, explore solutions, evaluate, act — and each stage's quality **caps** everything downstream: brilliant evaluation of options generated for the wrong problem still produces the wrong answer. Yet people invert the effort — seconds on definition, hours on debating solutions. The best decision-makers Parrish has watched do the opposite: they look *slow* at the front, because they refuse to solve anything until they're certain what they're solving — then move fast through the rest.

So the **definition principle**: the decision-maker owns the problem definition — *personally* — never by adopting the first framing offered. Inherited framings fail predictably, friend: they arrive as preferred solutions in costume, or describe only the part touching the speaker's department, or repeat last time's framing whether or not it worked. "Sales are down" is an *observation*. "Our onboarding loses 40 percent of trial users before they ever touch the core feature" — *that's* a problem you can attack. And mind the status trap: ego wants to skip to solutions because proposing solutions looks smart, while asking basic questions feels naive. In truth, the highest-leverage person in most rooms is the one willing to ask whether the group is solving the right problem — **a question that costs minutes and routinely saves months.**

## Attack the root, not the symptom

Symptom-solving is seductive: visible, immediate relief — *and a guarantee you'll solve the same problem again soon*, after it's compounded. It's painkillers for a condition that needs surgery, friend. And organizations institutionalize it, because they reward *firefighting*: the hero who handles the crisis gets promoted; the quiet soul who ensures the crisis never happens produces no drama and gets overlooked. So everyone learns to let fires start.

::: guess
Parrish offers one diagnostic question that drills beneath any presenting complaint to the root cause. If your problem is "we keep missing deadlines" — what's the question, and what does it surface?
---
**"What would have to be true for this problem not to exist in the first place?"** Ask it of the missed deadlines and up come the candidates: *our estimates are fantasy... scope changes mid-project... one team is a bottleneck* — each a different, more fundamental problem than the one you started with. Keep asking until the answers stop changing, friend: the first answer is usually *another symptom*, and the real cause sits two or three layers down — often somewhere *less flattering* than the surface story, which is exactly why ego steers people away from it. A team that frames its problem as "the market turned" never has to examine whether its product stopped being good. The payoff is gloriously asymmetric: solving a symptom buys you weeks; **solving a root cause deletes an entire recurring category of problems** — along with all the future time and attention they'd have eaten. And two cheap structural safeguards hold the door open: **write the problem down** — if you can't state it in a sentence or two, you don't understand it yet, and a written statement can be challenged, sharpened, and checked against later drift; and **hold two meetings** — one whose *only permitted output* is an agreed problem statement, no solutions allowed, then a solutions meeting at least a day later. It feels inefficient and is the opposite, friend: it blocks the loudest voice from framing and solving in one breath — and sleeping on a well-defined problem reliably beats brainstorming in the hour it was named.
:::

## Never accept a binary

Once the problem is defined, your decision can only be as good as the options you generated. Two disciplines raise the ceiling, friend. The **bad outcome principle**: don't just picture the futures you want — deliberately imagine each option failing, and work out what you'd do. It filters out options whose failure you can't survive, and pre-builds your crisis responses *while you're calm*, instead of improvising them with the emotion default in charge. And **second-level thinking** — the relentless *"and then what?"* The chocolate bar tastes good now; and then what? — a sugar crash, a small vote for a habit, a different body a year out. The option that looks best at level one — cut the price, ship the shortcut, dodge the hard conversation — is frequently the one that looks *worst* at level two or three.

::: guess
Now the trap of traps: someone presents you exactly TWO options. "Either we cut costs or we miss the quarter." What does a binary framing usually reveal about the person offering it — and what are Parrish's two escape moves?
---
**A binary usually means they stopped thinking early.** Either/or framings feel decisive, friend, but they're almost always artifacts of limited imagination, time pressure, or *someone's agenda*. Escape move one: **refuse to decide until you have at least three real options** — the requirement of a third breaks the spell, forcing genuine exploration instead of a coin flip dressed as analysis. And when you're stuck with the list in front of you, run the deeper trick: *"What would I do if I couldn't choose ANY of my current options?"* Deleting the whole list forces the search to restart — and the option generated under that constraint is frequently better than everything on the original list, which shows how fast inertia anchors us to whatever appeared first. Escape move two is the deepest: **dissolve the dilemma itself.** Both-and thinking treats either/or as a challenge: what would it take to get *both*? Often the answer is *sequencing* — a cheap, fast version of one option first to buy information — or a small experiment testing both paths, or a reframe revealing the two options were never actually exclusive. Parrish's observation, friend: exceptional decision-makers are distinguished less by how they choose between hard trade-offs than by how often they find **a secret third option that combines the best of both.** And remember the arithmetic: a mediocre choice from five strong options beats a perfect choice between two weak ones.
:::

## Before you go

So: a choice is not a decision, and most people make very few decisions; whoever speaks first frames the room, so own the definition — a problem statement is not a solution in costume; drill with "what would have to be true for this not to exist?" until the answers stop changing; write it down, split the meetings; imagine the failures and ask "and then what?"; and never, ever accept a binary. This week, friend: take one either/or you're facing and spend fifteen minutes designing the both-and.

But defining the problem and spreading the options only sets the table, friend. Tomorrow: how to actually *weigh* them — the way a great investor weighs a deal. The criteria that cut through noise; the invisible price tag on every yes, called opportunity cost; and the margin of safety — the buffer that lets you be wrong and still be fine. Next time: **evaluate like an investor.**

## Key takeaways

- A choice is reflexive; a decision requires conscious evaluation — and each stage's quality caps everything downstream.
- Own the framing: the first speaker's problem statement is usually a solution in costume, a department view, or last time's frame.
- Find the root with "what would have to be true for this problem not to exist?" — and keep asking until the answers stop changing.
- Two cheap safeguards: write the problem in two sentences, and separate the definition meeting from the solutions meeting.
- Generate options like a pessimist: imagine each option failing, and follow "and then what?" past the first level.
- Never accept a binary: demand three options, ask what you'd do if none were available, and hunt the both-and.
