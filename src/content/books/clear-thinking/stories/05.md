# Evaluate Like an Investor: Criteria, Opportunity Cost, Margin of Safety

Fifth night, friend — and it begins under a bridge.

Stand beneath any great span and ask the engineer a strange question: *how much weight can it carry?* The answer will puzzle you — because the bridge is built to bear **far more than any load it will ever officially face**. Trucks that will never be that heavy. Winds that may never blow. Crowds that will never gather. The engineers didn't miscalculate, friend. They built for a world that refuses to respect forecasts. They built a **margin of safety** — and tonight, we steal that idea, along with the rest of the investor's toolkit, for every decision you'll ever weigh.

## Criteria that pick a winner

![An engineer in a hard hat standing small beneath the massive steel underbelly of a great suspension bridge at golden hour, dwarfed by its overbuilt lattice of girders](/story-art/clear-thinking/05-bridge.jpg)

You've defined the real problem and spread out three-plus options. Now the weighing — and evaluation is only as good as the **criteria** you weigh against. Most people skip this and compare on gut feel, which means the four defaults do the choosing. Parrish gives criteria three non-negotiable properties: **clarity** — explainable to a twelve-year-old; "the option with the most synergy" decides nothing, because fuzzy language lets every option qualify. **Goal promotion** — each criterion must serve the actual objective, not a proxy; the hiring committee quietly optimizing for "candidate we'd enjoy lunch with" fails this test. And **decisiveness** — applied to your options, the criteria must crown *exactly one winner*. A tie doesn't mean the options are equal, friend. **A tie means you haven't yet decided what matters.** The enforcement is the **targeting principle**: name the *single* most important thing this decision must achieve and rank it above everything — because when everything is a priority, nothing is, and the trade-offs get made *by accident*. Write the criteria down *before* looking at options, so the options can't quietly rewrite the standards they'll be judged by.

Then feed the evaluation properly. **HiFi information** — high-fidelity, close to the source, because information degrades with every summary it passes through: run the cheap trial before committing; *go see for yourself* — the factory floor, the raw complaints — because dashboards are abstractions built by people with incentives; and keep your own opinion *quiet* while gathering, since the moment you announce a leaning, people tailor their input and your ego starts defending instead of testing.

::: guess
The other feed is HiEx — high-expertise — information. But imitators have mastered the vocabulary and confidence of expertise without the substance. Parrish gives five tells for unmasking them. How do you tell a real expert from an imitator?
---
**Ask them to explain it in plain language** — jargon is the imitator's camouflage, and requests to simplify expose them, while real experts delight in simplifying. **Ask fundamental questions** — imitators get frustrated or defensive; experts enjoy them. **Ask about failures** — imitators recite only wins; genuine experts carry scars and discuss them openly. **Ask what they've changed their mind about** — imitators can't answer, because their knowledge was *copied*, not earned through collisions with reality. And notice the volume, friend: imitators *broadcast* — names, credentials, titles — while experts let the quality of their reasoning carry the weight. Consult well, too: treat experts as instruments of your thinking, never replacements for it — give context, ask *how they'd think about* your problem rather than what to do, and remember their reasoning generalizes even when their bottom line doesn't. One last filter: **expertise is domain-specific** — a brilliant surgeon's opinion on your business strategy is a smart amateur's opinion. Weight advice by earned expertise *in this domain*, not by general status.
:::

## The invisible price tag

Every option's true cost includes what you *give up* to take it — the **opportunity cost** that appears on no invoice. Most bad deals look good, friend, only because nobody asked what else the resources could have done. Three questions surface the hidden ledger: **"Compared with what?"** — an option is never good or bad in isolation. **"And then what?"** — trace the consequences of consequences before pricing anything. **"At the expense of what?"** — name the sacrifice outright: the promotion at the expense of family dinners, the acquisition at the expense of flexibility. Convert costs into a common unit — money into hours of your life — and the incomparable ranks itself: a "free" board seat consuming 100 hours a year has a very visible price in your scarcest currency. And always put **"do nothing"** on the list as a real candidate, friend — the status quo is a choice, and sometimes it beats every active alternative. Acting for acting's sake is just the emotion and social defaults dressed as decisiveness. If nothing beats doing nothing... *wait*, keep gathering, and let better options emerge.

## The buffer that forgives you

Back to the bridge, friend — because now you can appreciate the investor's most beautiful borrowing.

::: guess
Parrish's margin-of-safety test is blunt and numerical. What's the question you must ask of any consequential option's worst case — and why isn't it just pessimism?
---
**Could you survive the worst case if it turned out TWICE as bad as you predict?** Job change assumes three months to find new work — could you survive *six*? Project's downside is $50,000 — could you take *$100,000* and stay in the game? The doubling isn't pessimism, friend — it's an honest correction for the fact that **your worst-case estimate was built from the same optimistic assumptions as your base case.** A margin of safety converts *"this should work"* into *"this works even when I'm wrong"* — the only kind of plan that survives contact with reality. Size it to the stakes: routine, recoverable decisions need little buffer — padding them wastes resources. But decisions touching *survival* — solvency, health, reputation, irreplaceable relationships — need generous ones, **because no expected upside compensates for ruin.** The math is gorgeously asymmetric: a margin costs a slice of best-case upside and eliminates the outcomes you cannot come back from. Cash runways, borrowing less than the bank approves, slack in the schedule — each trades a little efficiency for the right to be wrong and continue. And it loops back to Part One's great theme, friend: **the strong position isn't the one that wins the forecast. It's the one that survives the forecast being wrong.**
:::

One last gift: the clock. Split decisions on two axes — consequence and reversibility — and run opposite timers. Cheap, reversible calls: **as soon as possible** — extra deliberation buys nothing and clogs your attention. Consequential, irreversible ones: **as late as responsibly possible** — because time delivers information and you can't un-ring the bell. And three signals, any one sufficient, that deliberation is *over*: you've **stopped learning** (the inputs repeat; more research is anxiety in a productive costume); you're about to **lose an option** by not choosing; or you **already know** — and the hesitation is just the ego or social default shopping for cover. Certainty you refuse to act on, friend, decays into doubt.

## Before you go

So: criteria clear enough for a twelve-year-old, aimed at the real goal, ranked to crown one winner; HiFi from the source with your mouth shut, HiEx from experts screened for scars and changed minds; every yes priced against what it forecloses, with "do nothing" on the ballot; the twice-as-bad survival test standing guard over everything irreversible; and a clock that runs fast for the cheap calls, slow for the fateful ones. This week, friend: stress-test your biggest current commitment against a worst case twice as bad as forecast. If it wouldn't survive — add buffer *now*.

But choosing is still not doing, friend. Tomorrow, the final working chapter: execution — how plans meet reality and reality wins; the tripwires that catch drift before it becomes disaster; and the discipline that separates the truly great from the merely lucky — judging yourself by the *process*, never the outcome. ## Key takeaways

- Criteria must be clear (twelve-year-old test), goal-promoting (no proxies), and decisive — a tie means you haven't decided what matters.
- Targeting principle: rank ONE criterion above all — five equal priorities is a rudderless decision.
- Gather HiFi at the source with your opinion sheathed; screen HiEx with the imitator tells — plain language, scars, changed minds.
- Price the invisible: compared with what, and then what, at the expense of what — and let "do nothing" compete for real.
- Margin of safety: survive the worst case at twice the forecast — no upside compensates for ruin, and buffers keep bad luck from forcing your hand.
- Time it: reversible decisions ASAP, irreversible ones at the last responsible moment — and stop deliberating when learning stops, options expire, or you already know.
