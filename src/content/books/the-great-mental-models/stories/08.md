# Three Razors: Inversion, Occam, and Hanlon

Last night with this book, friend — and I promised you three blades. The first one was swung in public, on Easter Sunday, 1929, on Fifth Avenue in New York.

The American Tobacco Company had a problem: a powerful social taboo kept women from smoking in public, and half the population was therefore not buying Lucky Strikes. Every conventional marketer asked the forward question: *how do we advertise cigarettes to women?* Edward Bernays — the father of public relations — asked it **backward**: *what would society have to look like for women to want to smoke on their own?*

Hold that sinister little question, friend. It contains tonight's first tool — and a warning about what tools in the wrong hands can do.

## Invert, always invert

![Fashionable 1920s women in elegant coats and cloche hats striding confidently in an Easter parade on a grand New York avenue, onlookers and press cameras crowding the sidewalks](/story-art/the-great-mental-models/08-torches.jpg)

The mathematician Carl Jacobi cracked notoriously hard problems with one maxim: *"Invert, always invert."* Problems that resist a frontal assault often dissolve when restated in reverse. **Inversion** runs in two modes, friend. **Mode one: assume the conclusion and reason backward** — treat the goal as already real and ask what conditions would have to hold for it to exist; the vague "how do I get there?" becomes the concrete "what's missing?" **Mode two: define failure and avoid it.** You cannot *ensure* a great life — but you can reliably avoid the known wreckers: debt spirals, addictions, chronic dishonesty, neglected health. Failure modes are fewer and better documented than success paths. Most people only ever press harder on the accelerator; inversion says *check the brakes* — removing an obstacle is often cheaper and more durable than adding push.

::: guess
Back to Bernays. He never argued a single woman into smoking, and he never changed the product. Working entirely backward from "what would society have to look like?" — what did he actually do?
---
He **built the society**. He staged fashionable young women lighting cigarettes in the Easter parade and branded them **"torches of freedom"** — front-page news that reframed public smoking as feminist emancipation. He tied cigarettes to the slimness ideal — a substitute for sweets. And when women rejected Lucky Strike's green package for clashing with their wardrobes, he didn't change the package, friend — **he made green the fashionable color of the season**, enlisting designers, galas, and magazines. The environment was re-engineered until smoking *sold itself*. That is the terrifying leverage of backward thinking — and why it demands ethical restraint. But the same blade cuts clean in honest hands: John Bogle inverted "how do we beat the market?" into *"how do we avoid everything that makes investors underperform it?"* — strip the fees, the churn, the manager error — and out came the **index fund**. And Charlie Munger turned inversion into a life strategy: rather than striving for brilliance, work at being **consistently not stupid**. Brilliance is scarce, situational, unrepeatable; the catalog of standard stupidities — overconfidence, envy, leverage, ignored incentives — is *stable and learnable*. Amateurs win by seeking spectacular wins, friend. **Professionals win by not losing.**
:::

## Occam's blade

Second razor, honed by a fourteenth-century friar. **Occam's Razor**: when competing explanations fit the facts equally well, prefer the one with the **fewest assumptions**. And hear this clearly, friend — it isn't an aesthetic preference. *It's arithmetic.* Every explanation is a chain of assumptions; each assumption carries its own independent chance of being wrong; multiply the probabilities and a many-assumption story collapses fast, while the lean explanation stays standing. Medicine compressed it into an adage: **when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras.**

Carl Sagan showed the failure mode the razor guards against: a man claims a dragon lives in his garage. You propose a test — *ah, it's invisible*. Footprints? — *it floats*. Heat sensor? — *the fire is heatless*. Each patch saves the claim at the cost of another unverifiable assumption, until the theory **explains everything and predicts nothing** — the exact anatomy of every conspiracy theory ever built. But mind the razor's limits, friend: it's a *probability heuristic, not a law*. Some things are irreducibly complex — quantum mechanics is not simple, and nothing simpler matches its predictions. The test is never simplicity alone but simplicity *among explanations that fit all the evidence* — as Einstein put it, as simple as possible, **but no simpler**. When your theory needs a fresh patch every time it's challenged — that's not a theory to defend. That's one to discard.

## Hanlon's mercy

The third razor saves relationships — and once saved empires from themselves, when rulers remembered it. **Hanlon's Razor**: *never attribute to malice what is adequately explained by carelessness or incompetence.* The driver who cut you off is far more likely distracted than out to get you. This is Occam applied to human behavior, friend: incompetence needs **one** assumption — the person erred. Malice needs at least **two** — action *plus* intent, often a hidden motive as a third. Fewer assumptions, higher probability — and in the real world, error is simply far more abundant than villainy.

::: guess
Yet our guts scream malice anyway — and Kahneman and Tversky proved exactly why with a woman named Linda: 31, single, outspoken, philosophy major, deeply concerned with social justice. Subjects judged which was more probable: (a) Linda is a bank teller, or (b) Linda is a bank teller AND active in the feminist movement. What did most people answer — and why is it impossible?
---
**The overwhelming majority chose (b) — which is logically impossible.** A conjunction of two conditions can *never* be more probable than either condition alone — every "bank teller and feminist" is already a bank teller. This is the **conjunction fallacy**, friend: we judge probability by *narrative fit*, not logic — and a story with a villain and a motive feels MORE plausible precisely because it has more vivid detail... when every added detail makes it *less likely*. Assuming malice is the conjunction fallacy in the wild. And the cost can be historic: in 408 AD, Emperor Honorius read his brilliant general Stilicho's pragmatic negotiations with the Goths as *treachery* — and executed him. Thousands of Stilicho's soldiers defected to the Goth leader Alaric in disgust... **who sacked Rome two years later.** Assuming the worst manufactured the very betrayal Honorius feared. The razor is a default, not naïveté, friend — malice exists, and "mistakes" that always break one way deserve suspicion. But leading with charity keeps you rational, preserves what matters, and costs almost nothing when wrong.
:::

## The whole book in one breath

Nine tools now hang on your wall, friend. Six blind men taught us that every mind holds models, never reality — and Munger's latticework hangs many lenses together so their errors cancel. The map is not the territory: every model is a reduction, and the ground outranks the paper — ask Jane Jacobs, or the banks of 2008. The circle of competence matters for its *perimeter*, not its size — be the lifer, never the stranger who thinks he's one. First principles drill past "how it's done" to what's actually true — or you're building bamboo towers under an empty sky. Thought experiments run impossible laboratories in your skull — a young man chased a light beam and caught relativity. Second-order thinking asks *"and then what?"* — and never tears out a fence before learning why it stands. Probabilistic thinking estimates when certainty is impossible — priors, fat tails, and bets that can't ruin you. And three razors ride in your pocket: invert the problem, count the assumptions, and forgive the blunder before you suspect the knife. One sentence holds it all: **see the world through many lenses, know where each one ends, and let reality — never ego — be the referee.**

## Key takeaways

- Invert, always invert: assume the conclusion and reason backward, or define failure and eliminate it — check the brakes, not just the accelerator.
- Bernays' torches of freedom show inversion's terrifying leverage; Bogle's index fund and Munger's "consistently not stupid" show its honest power.
- Occam's Razor is arithmetic: every assumption is an independent chance to be wrong — hoofbeats mean horses, and endless patches mean a dead theory.
- Simplicity must still fit ALL the evidence: as simple as possible, but no simpler.
- Hanlon's Razor: incompetence needs one assumption, malice needs two or more — the Linda problem proves our story-loving minds get this backward.
- Honorius executed a loyal general on assumed malice and lost Rome — lead with charity; it costs almost nothing when wrong.
