# First Principles: Thinking from the Bedrock Up

Fourth night, friend — and it begins on a remote Pacific island, just after the Second World War.

For years, the islanders had watched a miracle: strange men cleared runways, built towers, spoke into little boxes — and giant silver birds descended from the sky, bellies full of food, tools, medicine, treasure. Then the war ended. The men left. The planes stopped.

So the islanders did something perfectly logical. They rebuilt *everything they had seen*: carved runways in the jungle, wooden control towers, headsets whittled from bamboo, fires positioned like landing lights. Every visible detail, faithfully reproduced. And then they waited for the planes.

**No planes came.** Hold that image all night, friend — the bamboo headset, the empty sky — because before we're done, you'll see the same ceremony performed in boardrooms, laboratories, and startups wearing very expensive clothes.

## Principles are few, methods are many

![Islanders standing beside a bamboo control tower and carved jungle runway at dusk, one wearing a carved wooden headset, all watching an empty sky](/story-art/the-great-mental-models/04-cargocult.jpg)

A **first principle** is a foundational proposition that stands on its own — it can't be deduced from anything else in your domain. Everything above it is method, convention, habit. As Harrington Emerson put it: methods *"may be a million and then some, but principles are few."* Master the principles and you can generate your own methods; memorize only methods, and you're stranded the moment conditions change.

And here's the honest confession, friend: most reasoning never touches bedrock. We reason **by analogy** — *this looks like that, copy that solution* — or **by convention** — *this is how our industry does it*. Both are efficient... and both quietly import every assumption, limitation, and error baked into the original. Analogies *borrow* conclusions. First principles *rebuild* them. Nor are first principles cosmic absolutes: they're relative to your problem. A physicist's bedrock is physical law; a manager's might be what people actually do under incentives. You needn't reduce a marketing question to quantum mechanics — but you *do* need to get beneath "best practices." The deepest obstacle is that our defaults are **invisible**: beliefs absorbed from parents, schools, employers, and culture feel like *perception*, not assumption. Other people's beliefs become the unseen walls of your thinking — and first principles work is the deliberate practice of making the walls visible, then testing whether they should stand.

## Two drills for reaching bedrock

The book hands you two interrogation tools. **Socratic questioning** — six disciplined steps: clarify what you actually think and why; challenge the assumptions (*what if the opposite were true?*); demand evidence and grade your sources; seek the perspective you're missing (*how do I know I, of all observers, am the correct one?*); trace the consequences if you're wrong; then loop back and question the question itself. Run a cherished belief through all six, friend, and one of two things happens: it earns its place near bedrock... or it dissolves into an assumption you were mistaking for knowledge. *Either outcome is a win.*

The second tool is the child's relentless *"but why?"* — promoted to engineering. The **Five Whys**: ask why, then ask why of the answer, five layers down.

::: guess
The Five Whys has a built-in test that tells you whether you've actually struck bedrock — or hit something else pretending to be it. What's the signal in your FINAL answer?
---
**Bedrock is a "what" or a "how" — something falsifiable and checkable.** *"The metal fatigues at this stress level." "Customers abandon carts when shipping costs appear late."* Land there and you have a foundation you can build on or fix. But if your chain terminates in *"because that's just how it's done," "because I said so,"* or a shrug — you haven't found a first principle, friend. **You've hit the wall of somebody's unexamined belief — quite possibly your own.** And that's precisely where the digging should continue. The two tools differ in grain: Socratic questioning is broad-spectrum, auditing a whole belief from six angles; the Five Whys is a drill — fast, narrow, perfect for tracing one recurring problem past its visible symptom to its root. Used regularly, they change your relationship with received wisdom itself: most of what anyone "knows" arrived secondhand and was filed away untested. These are the tools that sort the *load-bearing* knowledge from the decorative kind — before you stake a decision on it.
:::

## The planes don't land

Now, back to the island. Physicist Richard Feynman made it the centerpiece of his famous 1974 Caltech address, coining **cargo cult science**: work with all the visible trappings — apparatus, jargon, procedure — but missing the essential ingredient: *rigorous honesty about what the evidence does and doesn't show*, including bending over backwards to report how you might be wrong. Every form reproduced. **The planes don't land.**

And the warning generalizes everywhere, friend. The startup copying a famous company's open offices and free snacks. The investor mimicking Buffett's holdings without Buffett's *reasoning*. The manager installing "agile" ceremonies with no idea what feedback loops they exist to create. All of them are building bamboo control towers — reproducing *form* and wondering why *function* doesn't follow. Imitation is cheap precisely because it skips the expensive part: knowing **why**. But when you understand foundations, you can tell which parts of a practice do the causal work and which are ritual residue — and you can adapt when the world shifts, because you know which constraints are *real*. The cargo cultist has only one move: repeat the ceremony harder.

## From better cows to no cows

Here's the payoff, friend — the difference between improving and *inventing*.

::: guess
Take meat. Reasoning by convention, the problem is fixed: meat comes from animals, so better meat means better animals — breeding, feed, land. Now decompose meat to FIRST PRINCIPLES: what is meat, physically? And what door does that answer open?
---
**Muscle and fat tissue — particular proteins and cells in a particular structure.** Which means the animal was never the product, friend. *The animal is the historically available technology for assembling those cells.* See that clearly, and a brand-new question becomes possible: can the cells be grown **without the animal**? **Cultured meat** — real tissue cultivated directly from cells — is an option that simply *does not exist* inside the "improve the livestock" frame. It isn't a better cow. **It dissolves the need for the cow** — and with it the land, the water, the methane, the killing. And the pattern repeats through the whole history of invention: the nineteenth-century ice industry improved by analogy for decades — harvest lake ice faster! insulate the ships better! — until someone reasoned from what ice fundamentally *is*: a temperature state, not a New England crop. That reframe produced mechanical ice-making, then home refrigeration — and every such leap looked *absurd* to the incumbents, because it abandoned their premises instead of competing on them. That's the compounding return on the drills, friend: they expose which premises are physics... and which are habit. Never separate the two, and your ceiling is a slightly better version of what already exists. Sometimes that's enough. But when you need something genuinely *new*, first principles is the only road — everything else is remixing conclusions other people reached from foundations you never checked.
:::

## Before you go

So: principles are few and methods are many; analogy borrows while bedrock rebuilds; six Socratic steps and five whys drill past the wall of "that's how it's done"; bamboo towers under an empty sky wait wherever form gets copied without function; and the cow turned out to be a technology, not a necessity. This week, friend: take one stalled goal, split its constraints into two lists — *imposed by reality* and *imposed by convention* — and attack something on the second list.

But tomorrow the tools get stranger. There's a laboratory that needs no funding, no equipment, and no permission — it runs entirely inside your skull, and it produced some of the greatest discoveries in history. A sixteen-year-old boy once used it to chase a beam of light across the sky... and what he found there rewrote the universe. Next time: **thought experiments — the laboratory in your head.**

## Key takeaways

- First principles are the propositions that stand on their own; everything above them is method, convention, or habit.
- Analogy and convention are efficient but import every hidden error of the original — they borrow conclusions instead of rebuilding them.
- Socratic questioning audits a belief from six angles; the Five Whys drills until the answer is a checkable "what" or "how."
- "Because that's how it's done" isn't bedrock — it's the wall of an unexamined belief, and the signal to keep digging.
- Feynman's cargo cults: copy form without function — offices, ceremonies, holdings — and the planes never land.
- Meat is cells, the cow was the technology: restate what the product fundamentally IS, and invention replaces improvement.
