# Perpetual Beta: How Forecasters Improve

Sixth night, friend — and tonight the question that has hidden behind every campfire so far: **can this be learned?** Or were Doug Lorch and Bill Flack simply born seeing through fog?

Tetlock ran the numbers on everything — intelligence, numeracy, open-mindedness, political knowledge — hunting for the single strongest predictor of who *rises into* the superforecaster ranks and *stays* there. The winner wasn't a trait at all. It was a *practice* — a phrase borrowed from software engineering, where a program in beta is never finished: perpetually tested, debugged, updated.

**Perpetual beta.** The commitment to treat yourself the same way. And statistically, it was roughly **three times more powerful** a predictor than its closest rival.

## The investor who kept crashing

![A 1930s study with a man at a desk methodically marking corrections across scattered financial papers by lamplight, a rebuilt ledger open before him](/story-art/superforecasting/06-keynes.jpg)

That reframing is profound, friend: what elevates the best forecasters is less what they *are* than what they *do — and keep doing*. And the chapter's exemplar is a name you know: **John Maynard Keynes**. As an investor, the great economist was hammered repeatedly — nearly wiped out more than once in the 1920s. What made him die rich, with his college's endowment massively outperforming the market, was never that he stopped failing. It was that **every failure became a design change**: he dissected each disaster, abandoned his early faith in timing macroeconomic cycles, and rebuilt his whole strategy around a few businesses he deeply understood. Try, fail, analyze, redesign — for decades.

The psychology underneath has two names. Carol Dweck's **growth mindset**: the belief that abilities grow with effort — which turns failure from a *verdict* into *information*. Her experiments with children showed it's causal: kids praised for being *smart* chose easier puzzles and crumbled; kids praised for *effort* chose harder ones and improved. And Angela Duckworth's **grit**: passion and perseverance for long goals — the fuel that keeps the loop running through tedium and setbacks, predicting success from West Point to spelling bees better than raw ability. Superforecasters embody both: they *volunteer* to be scored mercilessly, treat bad Brier scores as puzzles rather than humiliations, and grind through unglamorous questions for months. If accuracy were a gift, the rational strategy would be hunting the gifted. Because it's a *growable skill*, the rational strategy is building people — and institutions — that learn.

## The confident officers who never improved

But here is the trap that swallows most of the world's "experience," friend — because effort alone improves nothing. Skill grows only from practice paired with **prompt, unambiguous feedback**. Remove the feedback, and practice becomes worse than useless.

::: guess
Research examined experienced police officers judging whether suspects were lying — people with years, sometimes decades, of interrogation experience. How did their lie-detection accuracy and their confidence compare to novices?
---
**Barely better than chance at detecting lies — and far MORE confident than novices.** Because an officer almost never learns, promptly and definitively, whether a particular suspect was actually lying. Years pile up *experience without verification* — and experience *feels* like expertise. The same trap holds physicians who never see their diagnoses resolved, and pundits whose predictions are never scored. Now contrast the professions where feedback is fast and merciless: **meteorologists** forecast in probabilities and meet the actual weather tomorrow — as a group, superbly calibrated. **Bridge players** bid their tricks and find out within minutes. Both genuinely improve with experience, because their environment tells them the truth quickly and often. Ordinary forecasting fails both feedback conditions at once: vague language ("could," "might," "serious possibility") leaves nothing crisp to check — and the months-long lag between forecast and outcome lets memory quietly mutate what was predicted. The fix is the tournament's whole secret: **precise probabilities, clear deadlines, written down, scored.** That's why its participants could improve at all.
:::

## The man who knew it all along

Even when outcomes *do* arrive, a subtler enemy corrupts the lesson — and it lives in your memory. Psychologist Baruch Fischhoff documented it with an elegant experiment in 1972, just before Nixon's historic trips to China and the Soviet Union.

::: guess
Fischhoff had people assign probabilities to possible outcomes — will Nixon meet Mao? will the US recognize the USSR? — then, after the trips, asked them to RECALL their own predictions. What had happened to their memories?
---
The memories had **shifted toward the outcomes**. Events that happened were "remembered" as having been given *higher* probabilities than they actually received; events that didn't were remembered lower. People had unknowingly *rewritten their own forecasts* to look more prescient — the "knew it all along" effect, **hindsight bias**, measured for the first time. And for anyone trying to learn from experience, this is devastating, friend: feedback only works if you can compare the *actual* forecast against the *actual* outcome — and hindsight corrupts the first term. If you "always knew" the startup would fail, there is no surprise, no error signal, *nothing to fix*. The bias flatters you precisely where you most need correction, converting genuine surprises into false confirmations. It's why casual forecasters stagnate for decades while tournament forecasters improved within *months*: a timestamped number can't be rewritten. The principle for your own life: **never trust your recollection of what you predicted. Trust the document.** Score yourself from the written record — always before re-reading your old reasoning.
:::

## The loop — and auditing your wins

Assemble the machine and improvement becomes a repeatable cycle: **try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again.** Precise forecast → compare against reality → diagnose the miss — wrong base rate? overweighted a vivid story? underreacted to diagnostic evidence? → change the *process* → run it again. Over hundreds of questions, small process corrections compound into large accuracy gains.

The step where most people flinch is the **postmortem** — and it's where superforecasters distinguish themselves twice over. First, they resist blaming bad luck for misses ("my reasoning was fine; the world misbehaved") because that forfeits the lesson — you know this move from Annie Duke's fire. But second, and rarer: **they audit their wins.** A forecast can be *right for the wrong reasons* — a lucky outcome validating a flawed process — and wrong for the right ones, when a sound 70% lands on the wrong side of maybe. Judging process rather than single outcomes is what keeps the feedback signal clean in both directions.

And one final humility, friend: much of the skill is **tacit** — know-how that words can't fully carry, like balancing a bicycle. No book, including Tetlock's, makes anyone a superforecaster by reading it — the tournament's training modules helped measurably, but the compounding gains went to those who *kept forecasting, kept scoring, kept adjusting*. The principles tell you what to practice. **Only scored repetitions build the skill.** So schedule the next rep now: this week's predictions, written as precise probabilities with deadlines on the calendar — and a postmortem waiting for each, hits included.

## Before you go

So: the strongest predictor of excellence is a practice, not a gift — perpetual beta, three times more powerful than anything else; growth mindset turns failure into information and grit keeps the loop turning; practice without feedback breeds confident officers who never improve; hindsight rewrites your memory so only the document can be trusted; and the loop must audit victories as ruthlessly as defeats.

## Key takeaways

- Perpetual beta — endless test-and-adjust — predicts superforecaster status three times more strongly than any other trait.
- Keynes crashed repeatedly and died rich: every failure became a design change — growth mindset plus grit is the engine.
- Practice without feedback builds confidence, not skill: experienced officers detect lies at chance level with maximal certainty.
- Meteorologists and bridge players improve because reality answers fast and clearly — give your forecasts numbers, deadlines, and scores.
- Hindsight bias rewrites your remembered predictions toward outcomes — trust the timestamped document, never the recollection.
- Run the loop: try, fail, analyze, adjust — and postmortem your wins too, because right-for-the-wrong-reasons corrupts learning.
