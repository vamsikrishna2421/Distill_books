# Granularity: Percentages, Not Fate

Fourth night, friend — and it opens in the most secure room in the world, at the highest-stakes meeting of a presidency.

Spring 2011. The White House Situation Room. Intelligence officers have tracked a tall man who paces the courtyard of a walled compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan. Is it Osama bin Laden? The President goes around the table asking his advisers for their probabilities. The answers range from roughly **30%... up to 95%**, with the CIA team leader near the top.

President Obama listens to all of it — months of analysis, satellite passes, courier networks — and then, by journalist Mark Bowden's account, delivers his summary: *"This is fifty-fifty. A coin flip."*

Hold that sentence, friend. Tonight is about what it reveals — about a dial in every human head with only three settings.

## The three-setting dial

![The White House Situation Room, advisers around the long table under low light, a screen glowing with a blurred satellite image of a walled compound](/story-art/superforecasting/04-situation.jpg)

Amos Tversky once joked that most people run on exactly three probability settings: **"gonna happen, not gonna happen, and maybe."** It's the evolutionary default — the tip-of-your-nose System 1 delivers confident verdicts, not confidence intervals; our ancestors needed *that shadow is a predator, run*, not *34% predator*.

But the three-setting dial throws away oceans of information. A 20% chance of war and a 45% chance of war are *different worlds* — and both collapse into "maybe." Superforecasters replace the dial with a full percentage scale, used with startling **granularity**: they distinguish 60% from 65%, 3% from 8%, where ordinary forecasters snap to 25-50-75.

::: guess
The obvious objection: surely that precision is theater — nobody can really tell 63% from 65% about a Nigerian election! Tetlock tested it directly, by taking years of tournament forecasts and ROUNDING them — to the nearest 5, then 10, then all the way down to yes/no/maybe. What happened to accuracy?
---
For *average* forecasters — nothing much. Rounding to the nearest tenth barely moved their scores; their granularity really was decoration. But for **superforecasters, every round of coarsening measurably damaged their Brier scores** — and collapsing them to the three-setting dial hurt badly. **Their decimal places are signal.** The fine distinctions carry real information; granularity, among the best, is a measurable component of skill, not a symptom of overconfidence. And here's the humanizing caveat, friend: this isn't because they're closet statisticians — almost none use equations or models. The numbers *express* carefully weighed judgment; they don't replace it. The skill is the habit of translating vague hunches into numbers that can be compared, updated, and *scored*. So this week's discipline: ban "will," "won't," and "maybe" from your forecasts. Every prediction gets a percentage — even when the precision feels uncomfortable. *Especially* then.
:::

## The wrong side of maybe

Why do smart people resist giving numbers? Because of how numbers get judged. A meteorologist says 70% chance of rain; the day dawns sunny; and everyone concludes *she was wrong*. She wasn't, friend — a 70% forecast explicitly reserves a 30% for sunshine. Convicting a single probabilistic call because the outcome landed on the *nearer side of 50* is the **wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy** — and it's how nearly everyone consumes forecasts.

The only fair test you already know from night two: calibration across *many* calls — does rain fall on about 70% of her 70% days? (For weather forecasters, with fast, unambiguous daily feedback: yes — they're among the best-calibrated professionals alive.) But watch the perverse incentive the fallacy creates: the pundit who says *"there is a serious possibility"* can never be caught on the wrong side of maybe — **because "serious possibility" has no side.** Vagueness is professionally safe and informationally worthless; the forecaster who commits to 70% gives you more and risks ridicule for it. Institutions that punish honest probabilities get elastic language in return — and then can't tell their good forecasters from their bad ones. And to the philosopher's objection — *"probabilities of unique events are meaningless; we never see the 30% of worlds where it rained"* — Tetlock's answer is a working one: treat probability as degree of belief, score it across many forecasts, and note that people who use the full scale *demonstrably outperform* those who don't. That's all the meaning a decision-maker needs.

Now return to the Situation Room. Estimates from 30 to 95, clustering well above half — flattened to *"fifty-fifty."* Read literally, that's the three-setting dial swallowing months of paid analysis whole. Tetlock offers the charitable reading — perhaps Obama meant *"debate won't settle this; decide anyway"* — and the raid succeeded. But the lesson stands at the apex of power, friend: **an organization whose leaders round everything to the nearest coin flip will get coin-flip-quality analysis, because nothing finer survives the meeting.** When someone hands you a 70%, consume it as 70 — not as "yes," and never as "maybe."

## Two kinds of maybe

Here is a distinction that will sharpen every estimate you ever make: **not all uncertainty is the same species.**

**Epistemic uncertainty** is about facts that *exist but are unknown*. Whether bin Laden stood in that compound was, in reality, a definite yes or no — the uncertainty lived only in the analysts' heads, and more intelligence could shrink it. **Aleatory uncertainty** is irreducible randomness: the weather three weeks out is not a hidden fact waiting to be found — the system itself is chaos, and no digging can pin it down.

The distinction is *operational*, friend. Facing epistemic uncertainty: **work the problem** — Fermi-ize, hunt diagnostic evidence, and let the probability march away from 50 as knowledge accumulates. Facing aleatory uncertainty: **humility** — keep the forecast near maybe, because precision about genuine dice is its own error. Superforecasters are granular where granularity is *earned*, and deliberately modest where the world rolls dice. Mistake one for the other and you get the two classic failures: lazy fifty-fifties on knowable questions... and false precision on unknowable ones. So before every forecast, one label: *is this a hidden fact, or true dice?* If you catch yourself at 50%, ask whether that number reflects analysis — or a dodged question. Then dig for an hour and see if it moves.

## Nothing is meant to be

And now the deepest obstacle to thinking in percentages — and it isn't innumeracy. It's *destiny*. Ask people about the pivotal events of their lives — meeting their spouse, the chance job, the near-miss — and so many say the same warm words: *"it was meant to be."*

::: guess
Tetlock gave his tournament forecasters a fate-and-destiny questionnaire — "everything happens for a reason," "events unfold according to a plan" — and correlated the answers with forecasting accuracy. What did he find?
---
**The higher a forecaster's fate score, the worse their accuracy.** Superforecasters rejected destiny-statements far more strongly than regular forecasters, who rejected them more than the general public — a clean staircase, running straight down into the Brier scores. And the mechanism is stark: if events are *fated*, probability is beside the point, evidence-weighing is theater, and hindsight always confirms that what happened had to happen. If events are *contingent*, percentages are the only honest language for the future — and every forecast is a claim that can be sharpened. The probabilistic retelling of "meant to be" is colder and, Tetlock argues, truer: your meeting was wildly improbable — *and wildly improbable things happen constantly*, because something improbable was bound to. Your own existence sits atop an absurd chain of contingencies, friend. That makes it **lucky** — which is somehow more precious than destined. Audit your own language this week: catch the fate-talk, and restate each "meant to be" as an improbable thing that happened anyway... to your enormous good fortune.
:::

## Before you go

So: the three-setting dial devours information — and even presidents flatten 30-to-95 into coin flips; the superforecasters' decimals are signal, proven by the rounding test; a 70% that goes the other way was never "wrong"; epistemic fog rewards digging while true dice reward humility; and destiny-thinking measurably corrodes foresight. Granularity, in the end, is a worldview: the world is not scripted, maybe comes in a hundred shades — and the job is to say *which* shade, then be scored on it.

But a forecast, once made, is not a monument, friend — it's a living thing. Tomorrow: the quiet superpower that separates the very best — *updating* — why the perfect revision is neither stubborn nor skittish; the man who beat the tournament by changing his mind in tiny increments, dozens of times per question; and the two opposite ways a forecaster dies: clinging too long, and jumping too fast.

## Key takeaways

- The three-setting dial (yes/no/maybe) is the evolutionary default — and it discards the very information forecasting exists to produce.
- The rounding test: superforecasters' decimal places are signal — coarsening their numbers measurably damaged accuracy.
- The wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy punishes honest 70%s and rewards uncatchable vagueness — judge calibration across many calls.
- Even presidents flatten ranges into coin flips: consume a 70% as 70, and never average expert estimates down to "maybe."
- Label the uncertainty: epistemic (hidden fact — dig) versus aleatory (true dice — stay humble near maybe).
- Fate-belief predicts bad forecasting: restate "meant to be" as "wildly improbable, and it happened anyway — lucky me."
