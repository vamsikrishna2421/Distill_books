# Updating: Many Small Steps, Few Big Leaps

Fifth night, friend — and it begins on a sailboat. Watch a good sailor hold a course: not one grand turn of the wheel, but a hundred tiny corrections, each almost invisible — a nudge for every gust, a touch for every swell. The boat never points perfectly; it *converges*.

Tonight's discovery is that the best forecasters on earth sail exactly this way. A forecast, once made, is not a monument. It is a live position on a moving sea — and the quiet superpower that separates the top 2% is not brilliance at the first guess. It is **the art of revision**.

## The sailor's style

![A lone sailor at the wheel of a small yacht making a tiny course correction, calm sea at dawn, distant landmark on the horizon](/story-art/superforecasting/05-sailor.jpg)

The tournament data is blunt: how often a forecaster updated was among the *strongest predictors of accuracy*. But here's what the news-junkie theory misses: the updates themselves were characteristically **tiny** — nudges of three or four percentage points, not lurches from 20 to 80. Tim Minto, one of the tournament's very best (Brier ≈ 0.15), revised a single question about Syrian refugee numbers *dozens of times*, almost always by small increments — each revision individually unimpressive, collectively keeping his probability lashed tight to reality.

Two caveats keep this honest, friend. First: **updating cannot rescue a careless start** — superforecasters' *initial* estimates were already about 50% more accurate than regular forecasters', so the Fermi-izing and base-rate work of night three still does the heavy lifting. Second: frequency isn't the skill — anyone can fiddle with numbers daily. The skill is judging *when* news warrants movement and *how much*. And there's quiet math behind the small steps: every update carries some error, and many small steps let the errors *partially cancel* — while one dramatic swing bets everything on a single reading of ambiguous evidence. Gradualism is a hedge against your own fallibility.

The whole art, then, is threading between two opposite deaths. Let me show you both.

## Death by clinging

The first death is **belief perseverance** — clinging after the evidence collapses. Its most spectacular case study is Leon Festinger's doomsday cult: when the prophesied apocalypse *failed to arrive*, the committed members didn't abandon the belief — they concluded their faith had *saved the world*. The everyday versions are gentler and everywhere: we rationalize, reinterpret, and simply fail to see.

Two forces supercharge it. **Commitment**: once you've stated a forecast — publicly, professionally — revising feels like confessing error, so the stakes stop being accuracy and become reputation. The more invested, the more evidence needed to move you — *exactly backward*. And **identity**: some beliefs aren't estimates at all but tribal badges — Kahan's gun-control experiment showed more numeracy producing *more* polarization when the numbers threatened the tribe. Intelligence as a weapon for the tribe, not a tool for the truth.

The superforecaster's defense is almost spiritual, friend: **keep your self-worth out of your forecasts.** A forecast is a provisional probability, not a possession. Write your numbers down privately before announcing anywhere, and treat every stated figure as a draft under revision. The attitude is the famous line attributed to Keynes — *"When the facts change, I change my mind. What do you do, sir?"* — probably apocryphal, and true anyway.

## Death by twitching

The mirror-image death: treating every headline as signal. Most daily fluctuation — the poll wobble, the stray remark, one month's data — is *noise around an unchanged reality*, and chasing it makes you the over-trading investor who churns his portfolio on rumors and loses to the patient index.

::: guess
Worse still: researchers Zukier and Nisbett asked people to predict a student's GPA. One group got only diagnostic facts (his grades in related work). Another got the same facts PLUS useless details — his hobbies, how many plants he kept. What did the irrelevant information do?
---
It **diluted the diagnostic facts** — dragging predictions toward a mushy average and making judgments measurably *worse*. More information degraded accuracy, because the mind weighs the whole impression instead of filtering for relevance. That's the **dilution effect**, and it means the forecaster's tension is real: stay hungry for information, yet hold steady against noise. The resolution is a single filter question applied to every incoming item: **does this change the probability of the outcome — or does it merely change how the situation *feels*?** A vivid, emotional, non-diagnostic story should move you *zero*. A dry, boring, diagnostic statistic may deserve real weight. And the small-step habit doubles as insurance: cap most moves at a few points, and no single noisy headline can whipsaw you — while genuine signal still accumulates across many updates.
:::

## Bayes in spirit

Both deaths are errors of *weighting* — and the discipline that fixes weighting is three centuries old. **Bayes' theorem**, from the eighteenth-century minister Thomas Bayes: your new belief should equal your prior belief moved in proportion to the **diagnosticity** of the evidence — how much more likely that evidence would be if one hypothesis were true rather than the other. Highly diagnostic: big move. Ambiguous: small move. Irrelevant: *no move*. Superforecasters almost never plug numbers into the formula, friend — they've internalized its spirit.

Watch it work under fire, in the hands of Bill Flack — the polonium man from night three, holding his ~60% on whether the *Swiss or French* teams would find polonium in Arafat's remains.

::: guess
Mid-question, a bombshell: the head of the RUSSIAN forensic team announced Arafat's remains showed NO polonium traces. Headlines everywhere; forecasters slashed their probabilities. What did Flack do — and why?
---
He **barely moved** — and his reasoning is the whole chapter in miniature. The question asked specifically about the *Swiss or French* investigations — not the Russian one. The Russian statement was ambiguous, possibly politically shaded, and said little about what the *named* teams would report. Loud? Extremely. Diagnostic? **Barely.** So Flack adjusted only modestly... and weeks later the Swiss team reported polonium levels moderately supporting the poisoning hypothesis. The question resolved *yes*; Flack's disciplined weighting earned an excellent score, and the overreactors paid full price for confusing volume with evidence. Burn the rule in, friend: **the size of an update should track the evidential weight of the news — never its volume, vividness, or emotional charge.**
:::

## When the right move is a leap

But gradualism is a default, not a dogma — and here is the twist that completes the skill. Bayes cuts both ways: when evidence is *genuinely, powerfully* diagnostic, a polite little adjustment is just underreaction wearing prudence as a costume. Superforecasters occasionally jump 30, 40, 50 points — and the *ability* to leap separates them from forecasters who mistake stubbornness for steadiness.

Flack again. Asked whether Japan's prime minister Abe would visit the controversial Yasukuni Shrine that year, he built a tidy rational-actor model: a visit would inflame China and South Korea and irritate Washington for negligible gain — a rational leader sends a ritual offering instead. Probability: low. Then a news report indicated Abe *actually planned to visit*. Everything belief-perseverance whispers said: explain it away, protect the model. Instead Flack asked the only question that matters — *is this diagnostic?* Direct, credible reporting about the specific event in question: **maximally**. He overrode his own elegant model and jumped sharply upward. Abe visited the shrine that December.

That's the full skill in one man, friend: hold your analysis firmly enough to resist noise... and loosely enough to abandon it the moment reality dissents. Many small steps, because most news deserves only a nudge. Few big leaps, reserved for evidence that truly discriminates. Both serve one master: **proportioning belief to evidence.**

## Before you go

So: the best forecasters sail — constant tiny corrections, initial-estimate quality still doing half the work; clinging is death by commitment and tribe; twitching is death by dilution; Bayes' spirit sizes every move to diagnosticity — and when the evidence is direct and credible, the leap *is* the discipline.

But one question has been hiding behind all five nights, friend: can this be *taught*? Or are superforecasters simply born? ## Key takeaways

- Update frequency predicts accuracy — but the signature move is a 3-4 point nudge, and gradualism lets errors cancel.
- Updating can't rescue a careless start: initial estimates still carry half the skill.
- Death by clinging: commitment and identity make evidence feel like attack — keep self-worth out of your forecasts.
- Death by twitching: irrelevant details dilute diagnostic facts — ask "does this change the probability, or just the feeling?"
- Bayes in spirit: move in proportion to diagnosticity — Flack ignored the loud Russian headline and scored.
- Leap when evidence is direct and credible about the exact question — stubbornness is not steadiness.
