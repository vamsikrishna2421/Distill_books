# Caching: Forgetting as a Feature

Fourth night, friend — and it begins with a young man fleeing Hungary in 1956, carrying almost nothing.

Everything he owned, reduced to what two hands could hold — a man given brutal early practice in the one skill this night is about: *deciding what to keep*. His name was László Bélády. Ten years later, at IBM, he would publish the definitive mathematics of exactly that problem — a paper so essential it became, for over a decade, **the most cited paper in all of computer science**.

Because it turns out, friend, that "what do I keep close, and what do I let go?" isn't just the refugee's question. It's the question inside your computer, your closet, your desk... and — by the end of tonight — your own memory. And the answer will forgive you for things you've spent years apologizing for.

## Small, fast, and close

![A young man in a 1950s coat carrying a single worn suitcase along a misty night road toward a distant border light](/story-art/algorithms-to-live-by/04-belady.jpg)

Computers face a brutal trade-off: memory can be *fast* or *plentiful* — never both, at any sane price. The solution, sketched by von Neumann's team in 1946 and named at IBM in 1969, is the **cache**: a small, quick store that automatically keeps copies of whatever you pulled most recently from the big, slow warehouse behind it. The gamble works because of **locality of reference** — programs and people don't touch their things uniformly; they return to the same items in bursts. A cache holding *one percent* of the data can serve the vast majority of requests at full speed. And the architecture is fractal, friend: multiple cache levels inside the chip, RAM caching the disk, the disk caching the network — and outside the machine, *your desk caches your filing cabinet, your closet caches your basement, your bag caches your home*. The design question is never "how do I store everything?" It's **"what belongs in the small, fast layer right now?"** — which makes the real problem *deciding what to remove*.

::: guess
That's eviction — the cache is full, something must go. Bélády proved the perfect rule: evict the item you'll need again FURTHEST in the future. One problem: it requires clairvoyance. So of the realistic policies — evict at random, evict the oldest arrival (FIFO), or evict the LEAST RECENTLY USED (LRU) — which comes closest to perfection, and what does that say about your instincts?
---
**LRU — and it isn't close.** Toss out whatever has gone *untouched* the longest, and you approach Bélády's clairvoyant ideal, usually beating FIFO by a wide margin. The engine is **temporal locality**: if you used something recently, you'll probably use it again soon. And notice, friend, how LRU quietly *inverts* a common intuition — we tell ourselves the thing we haven't needed in ages is "due," about to become relevant any day now. The data say the opposite: **the longer something has sat unused, the longer it will likely keep sitting.** The recent past is the best available forecast of the near future. One heuristic — *recency as the proxy for the unknowable* — powers your browser's back button, your phone's app switcher, and, as we're about to see, the best filing system ever accidentally invented. And caching's other dimension is *distance*: if you can't make data travel faster, **move it closer before it's asked for**. That's Netflix pre-loading its most-watched titles onto drives inside your internet provider — most of what the world streams is served from a cache down the street. It's Amazon's warehouses stocked by regional prediction (they patented shipping things *toward you before you buy them*) — warehouses that often skip tidy categories entirely for **chaotic storage**, items shelved wherever space exists, tracked by computer. External order matters less than retrieval speed, friend. At home: daily things at arm's reach, monthly things in the closet, yearly things in the basement — a full memory hierarchy in drywall.
:::

## The box that files itself

Now meet Japanese economist Yukio Noguchi, who was drowning in documents when he invented what he called the "super" filing system. Every document goes in an envelope; all envelopes stand in one box. New ones enter *at the left end*. And here's the whole trick, friend: when you *use* a file, you don't return it to its "proper" place — **you put it back at the left end too.** Search always starts from the left.

Watch what happens: the box *organizes itself*. The files you actually use hover near the left, found in seconds; dormant files drift steadily rightward, sorting themselves into deep storage — and quietly *nominating themselves for disposal* — with zero effort from you. Noguchi thought it was a pragmatic hack. Computer scientists recognized the **move-to-front rule**, and in 1985 Sleator and Tarjan *proved* it: against any sequence of requests, move-to-front stays within a constant factor of the best arrangement a clairvoyant could choose. Noguchi's box is as good as filing gets without foreknowledge — because it **is** LRU. Now tip the box on its back, friend... and you're looking at **the pile of papers on your desk.** A pile you pull from and return to the top is a move-to-front list: relevant documents float, irrelevant ones sink. Far from a guilty failure of discipline, *the pile is a well-engineered cache* — and alphabetizing it would probably make retrieval slower. The wardrobe version: hang worn clothes back at one end of the rod, and six months later the far end has honestly compiled your donation bag.

## The forgetting that isn't failure

The last move is the most personal. Carnegie Mellon psychologist John Anderson asked a radical question in the late 1980s: instead of asking how memory *fails* — what problem is it optimally *solving*?

::: guess
With Lael Schooler, Anderson measured the statistics of the world memory has to serve — years of New York Times headlines, parents' speech to children — tracking how a word's probability of coming up again declines with time since its last appearance. Then he laid that curve beside the human forgetting curve psychologists had measured since Ebbinghaus. What did the comparison show?
---
**They're essentially the same curve.** The rate at which memories become harder to retrieve *mirrors the rate at which the world stops asking for them*, friend. Recent items are overwhelmingly likely to recur; long-absent ones almost never do — and human recall fades on precisely that schedule. Your memory isn't leaking. **It's running LRU against an accurately estimated environment** — keeping the likely-needed instantly available and letting the unlikely-needed sink toward the back of Noguchi's box. Forgetting is the eviction policy, and it's *tuned to reality*. The same analysis re-frames aging with unexpected tenderness: linguist Michael Ramscar showed that much of older adults' slower recall is predicted simply by **database size** — decades more names, words, and facts mean a vastly larger archive to search. The longer lookup is a signature of accumulated knowledge, *not decay* — and the tip-of-the-tongue stall is just a cache miss while the request travels to deeper storage. The item isn't gone, friend. It's in the warehouse. So work *with* the design: availability tracks recency, so **pre-warm your cache** — glance at your notes before the meeting, rehearse the names on the way to the party. And forgive yourself the things you haven't touched in years. By the world's own statistics... you almost certainly weren't going to need them.
:::

## Before you go

So: a refugee's question became computing's most-cited paper; the cache wins by keeping the small, fast layer honest, and LRU — keep what was touched last — is as close to clairvoyance as mortals get; distance is latency, so move things toward their point of use; Noguchi's box and your desk pile are provably near-optimal; and your forgetting is a feature, tracking the exact statistics of what the world will ask you for. This week, friend: when you clear any space — closet, phone, inbox — evict by LRU and nothing else. Sentiment lies; recency doesn't.

But knowing *what to keep close* only sharpens the harder question, friend: with the day already too short, *what do you do first?* Tomorrow the computer scientists take on the to-do list itself — and the results are startling: the rule that provably minimizes how late you'll be; the rule that clears the most items fastest; why doing the easiest thing first is mathematically defensible *and* psychologically seductive; and the day NASA's Mars Pathfinder froze on another planet because of a scheduling bug your own life has too. Next time: **scheduling — what to do, and in what order.**

## Key takeaways

- Every storage system is a hierarchy: small-fast in front, big-slow behind — the design question is what earns the front.
- Bélády's optimum needs clairvoyance; LRU is the mortal champion — evict whatever has gone longest untouched.
- The "it's due to be useful" instinct is backwards: the longer unused, the longer it'll stay unused.
- Distance is latency: CDNs, Netflix drives, and Amazon's anticipatory shipping all move things closer before they're wanted — copy that at home.
- Noguchi's move-to-front box is provably near-optimal — your desk pile is a working cache, not a character flaw.
- Human forgetting matches the world's own recurrence statistics: it's LRU tuned to reality — pre-warm what you'll need, forgive the rest.
