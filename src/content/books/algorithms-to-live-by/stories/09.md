# Living Among Other Optimizers: The Last Lesson

Last night with this book, friend — and it begins in October 1969, in a lab at UCLA, where the internet is about to be born... and immediately crash.

Leonard Kleinrock's team is sending the very first message over the ARPANET to Stanford. The word is "LOGIN." They type **L** — it arrives. They type **O** — it arrives. They type **G** — *and the whole system crashes.* The first message ever carried by the network that would swallow the world was **"LO"** — as in *lo and behold*, a fittingly humble start.

Everything until tonight, friend, was about optimizing alone: your search, your schedule, your predictions. The final chapter asks the harder question: what happens when the world is full of *other optimizers* — flaky partners, selfish drivers, rival bidders — and no one is in charge? The internet's answer, it turns out, is a philosophy of life.

## Building trust on an unreliable channel

![A 1969 computer lab at night, engineers in shirtsleeves gathered around a refrigerator-sized terminal, one typing while the others lean in under fluorescent light](/story-art/algorithms-to-live-by/09-arpanet.jpg)

The internet abandoned the phone company's dedicated lines for **packet switching**: messages diced into packets that each find their own route — a "connection" is just a *consensual fiction* maintained by the two ends. Reliability comes from the humble **acknowledgment**: every packet confirmed, everything unconfirmed re-sent. But certainty itself is provably out of reach, friend — the **Byzantine generals problem** shows two armies coordinating by capturable messengers can *never* be sure, because the last message sent is always unconfirmed. Networks — and people — proceed anyway, replacing certainty with good-enough protocols. And the most elegant trick: **making silence speak.** A missing acknowledgment *is* a message — the network is full, slow down. Non-response is data, friend, and its meaning depends on how long you'd expect a reply to take.

::: guess
1971: ALOHAnet links the Hawaiian islands by radio — but when two transmitters talk at once, both messages die, and if both retry immediately, they collide forever. The fix now underlies all of Wi-Fi... and it doubles as the mathematically sound policy for dealing with people who let you down. What is it?
---
**Exponential backoff**: after a failure, wait a *random* delay — and after each further failure, **double** the maximum wait. Never give up entirely; never hammer. It's every "try again later" on the internet, friend, and its human translation is a policy for flaky friends and broken trust: **infinite mercy with exponentially decaying expectation.** You never write someone off — but after each letdown, you invest less and wait longer before trying again. A middle path between naive endless chances and brittle one-strike rules. Its cousin runs your bandwidth: TCP's **Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease** — push one packet faster per success, and the instant one drops, *cut your rate in half*. The result is the eternal **sawtooth**: probing up, backing off — because the only way to find an unknowable ceiling is to bump into it. Ant colonies regulate foragers with the identical feedback loop (the "anternet"), and as a career philosophy it rebuts the Peter Principle: push upward until strain shows, pull back *promptly and without stigma*, resume. And beware **bufferbloat**, friend: buffers that never say no destroy responsiveness — the router with an always-full queue delivers everything *late*, and so does the always-full calendar. Sometimes the kindest signal is an explicit **no** — a visible bounce instead of a silent weeks-long queue.
:::

## When everyone plays their best — and everyone loses

Enter the other optimizers. A **Nash equilibrium** is a set of strategies where no one gains by changing course alone — and Nash proved every game has one. But existence isn't comfort, friend. Computing equilibria is provably intractable ("if your laptop cannot find it, neither can the market") — and worse, an equilibrium can be *stable and terrible at once*. The **prisoner's dilemma**: defection is each player's best move regardless of the other... and mutual defection leaves both worse off than cooperation. Scale it up and you get the **tragedy of the commons** — pastures, climates, and *unlimited vacation policies*, where each employee rationally takes slightly less than their peers to look dedicated, and the ratchet grinds toward no vacation at all. The gap between equilibrium and optimum has a name — the **price of anarchy** — and sometimes it's mercifully small: selfish drivers cost at most a third over perfect central routing, which is why self-driving cars won't abolish traffic. But sometimes it's perverse: **Braess's paradox** — *adding* a road can slow everyone down, and closing one can speed them up.

::: guess
So if the game's equilibrium is bad, playing better is useless — deviate alone and you're the sucker. The real move is mechanism design: change the RULES. The masterpiece is William Vickrey's auction. Sealed bids, highest bidder wins — but what does the winner PAY, and what miracle does that one tweak produce?
---
**The winner pays the SECOND-highest bid.** And under that one rule, friend, the dominant strategy — the provably best move for every bidder — is to **bid exactly what the thing is truly worth to you.** Overbid and you risk paying more than your value; underbid and you only risk losing something you'd happily have won at the price. Honesty stops being a virtue and becomes the *winning strategy* — no bid-shading, no information cascades where rational bidders herd each other's prices away from reality. Roger Myerson's **revelation principle** generalizes the miracle: *any* game that rewards strategic lying can be redesigned into an equivalent game where truth-telling is optimal. **Good rules don't demand virtue, friend — they make virtue the winning move.** The same logic runs through mob bosses (a credible punisher makes silence rational), mandated vacations (a rule frees everyone from a race no one wanted), and — Robert Frank argues — your own emotions: anger is an involuntary commitment device that makes retaliation *credible*, and love solves the couple's endless optimal-stopping problem by making defection unthinkable. Being visibly, reliably a little irrational can beat being a transparent calculator.
:::

## Computational kindness

The book's final gift is an ethic. Computation — searching, weighing, deciding — is genuinely *costly*, so imposing it on others is a real harm, and absorbing it for them is a real gift. *"Whenever works for you!"* sounds polite, friend — but it hands your friend the entire scheduling search problem. *"Tuesday noon, or Wednesday morning?"* is kinder, because **verifying an option is vastly easier than generating one**. State your preferences instead of feigning indifference; the bus stop's countdown clock and the garage's open-space counter are the same mercy at city scale. And the kindness runs inward: most of life's real problems are provably intractable, so *the optimal procedure fails most of the time* — the 37% rule loses 63% of it. Judge yourself by **process, not outcome**, friend. If you used the best strategy available, a bad result was never a mistake.

## The whole book in one breath

Nine nights, nine tools. Kepler taught you when to stop looking — 37%, then leap. The bandits taught you to explore early and exploit late, and that grandmother's small circle is optimal play. Carroll caught the silver medal lying, and the messy desk was vindicated by the sort-search trade-off. Bélády's suitcase became the cache, and your forgetting turned out to be tuned to reality. Mars froze on a priority inversion, and the chore blocking your big project inherited its priority. Gott doubled the Berlin Wall's age, and your gut turned out to be running Bayes on whatever data life fed it. Darwin's single page beat the nine-factor model — think less when the data is thin. Lincoln's circuit was provably unsolvable, so relax the rules and roll honest dice. And tonight: build trust on an unreliable channel, forgive with exponential backoff, change bad games instead of playing them harder — and be computationally kind. One breath shorter still, friend: **use the best process the problem allows, and forgive the outcomes — everyone you meet is running heuristics against problems too hard for anyone to solve exactly.**

## Key takeaways

- Reliability without certainty: acknowledgments, retries, and silence-as-signal — proceed on good-enough protocols, as proven perfection is impossible.
- Exponential backoff is machine forgiveness: never write off, but double the wait and shrink the stakes after each letdown.
- Live AIMD: push until strain shows, cut back sharply without stigma — and keep buffers for bursts, never as permanent backlogs.
- Equilibria can be stable and awful (prisoner's dilemma, vacation ratchets); the price of anarchy measures the damage.
- Don't play bad games better — redesign them: Vickrey's second-price rule makes honesty the dominant strategy.
- Computational kindness: offer options instead of "whatever works," state preferences plainly, and judge everyone — including yourself — by process, not outcome.
