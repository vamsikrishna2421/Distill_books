# Explore/Exploit: New Favorite or Old Reliable

Second night, friend — and it begins with a mathematical problem so vicious that during the Second World War, Allied analysts joked about *dropping it on Germany* — as intellectual sabotage, to sap the enemy's finest minds the way it had sapped theirs.

The monster looks innocent: a casino row of slot machines — "one-armed bandits" — each paying off at a different, *unknown* rate. Every pull is simultaneously a bet and an experiment: it wins or loses money *and* teaches you about that machine. Which arm do you pull? A machine that's paid 9 times in 15? Or the mysterious one that paid once in two pulls — worse record, but barely tested, so who knows?

That, friend, is the **multi-armed bandit problem** — and you play it every single day. The favorite restaurant or the new one? The old friends or the new invitation? The proven career or the untested door? Tonight: what the mathematicians finally learned when they cracked it.

## The interval makes the strategy

![A lone figure in a dim vintage casino standing before a long row of glowing slot machines receding into darkness, one machine shining brighter than the rest](/story-art/algorithms-to-live-by/02-bandits.jpg)

First, the vocabulary — and notice it carries no moral charge. **Exploration** is gathering information about your options. **Exploitation** is *using* the information you have to collect the best known payoff — and savoring a beloved restaurant with old friends is exploitation in its finest form. The earliest practical rule, from Herbert Robbins in 1952 — *Win-Stay, Lose-Shift* — provably beats chance, but it panics after a single loss and ignores the variable that turns out to be the whole heart of the matter: **how much time you have left.**

Because here's the master key, friend: *information is only valuable if you have future opportunities to use it.* A new arrival in a city should try every promising restaurant; someone moving away next week should book the favorites. **Explore early, exploit late.** The lens explains Hollywood with eerie precision: sequels are pure exploitation, and among the year's top-ten grossing films there were two sequels in 1981... three in 1991... five in 2001... **eight in 2011** — a studio system with declining ticket sales behaving exactly like a gambler who believes the casino is about to close. Rational — and a confession of expected decline. The same lens redeems toddlers: psychologist Alison Gopnik argues childhood *is* the explore phase — a long protected interval where flitting attention and relentless novelty-seeking are precisely what an algorithm at the start of a very long game should do. Toddlers aren't bad at focusing, friend. **They're superb at exploring.**

## A price on the unknown

In the 1970s, John Gittins — hired by Unilever to figure out how to allocate research money among drug projects — found what decades of mathematicians had missed: an *exact solution* to the bandit problem. Assume each future payoff is worth some constant fraction of the one before — say 99% — and every arm can be assigned a single number, the **Gittins index**. Optimal play becomes childishly simple: always pull the arm with the highest number.

::: guess
Here's the shocking part. With payoffs discounted at 90% per pull, the Gittins index of a machine you know NOTHING about — never pulled once — is 0.7029. What does that number mean in practice?
---
**A completely untested machine is worth more than a machine you KNOW pays 70% of the time.** Sit with that, friend: total ignorance, rationally preferred to a proven, excellent option. Why? Because *uncertainty itself has value* — the unknown machine might be wonderful, and if it isn't, you simply walk away. The mathematics builds in a genuine **exploration bonus**: a machine that's paid 1-in-2 outranks one that's paid 9-in-18 despite identical rates, because the smaller sample leaves more upside. But Gittins' optimality is fragile — it assumes tidy discounting and *free switching*, and once switching costs money (jobs, cities, houses), sticking with known-good gains real appeal. So the modern approach flips the objective: minimize **regret** — the gap between what you earned and what the best arm would have paid from the start. In 1985 Lai and Robbins proved the beautiful floor: regret *must* grow under any strategy... but the best strategies make it grow only **logarithmically** — as many mistakes in your first ten pulls as in your next ninety, fewer new errors every year of your life. The champion algorithms are **Upper Confidence Bound**: rank every arm not by its average but by *the best it could plausibly be* — the top of its confidence interval. **Optimism in the face of uncertainty**, friend: the unknown gets the benefit of the doubt, because options are only ever eliminated by *evidence* — never by pessimism. It's the engine inside modern recommendation systems... and a fine rule for accepting invitations.
:::

The web runs on this math now: **A/B testing** shows different users different versions and shifts traffic toward winners — Google once tested forty-one shades of blue; the 2008 Obama campaign discovered "Learn More" beat "Sign Up" and lifted sign-ups 40%, worth roughly $60 million. But medicine raises the stakes to lives: standard clinical trials split patients 50/50 *to the very end* — pure exploration long after the data favor one arm. Biostatistician Marvin Zelen proposed **play-the-winner** in 1969: every success adds a ball to that treatment's side of the hat, tilting later patients toward what's working. Then came **ECMO** — a Michigan team's oxygenation machine for newborns in respiratory failure. Zelen-style trial: one conventionally treated infant died; *eleven straight ECMO infants lived*. Skeptics demanded more: in the compromise Harvard trial, **four of ten** conventional infants died, all nine ECMO infants lived. Still unconvinced, the field ran a traditional UK trial in the '90s — during which roughly **twice as many infants died** in the conventional arm before ECMO was finally accepted. Statisticians argue those deaths were the avoidable price of ignoring bandit algorithms, friend. Sometimes the explore/exploit balance is a matter of arithmetic. Sometimes it's a matter of mercy.

## The rationality of old age

One more gift tonight — and it may change how you see your grandparents. Stanford psychologist Laura Carstensen asked why older people's social circles shrink... and rejected the sad standard story of decline and loneliness.

::: guess
Carstensen ran an experiment: choose a companion for thirty minutes — a close family member, the author of a book you've read, or an interesting new acquaintance. Older people overwhelmingly chose family; the young chose novelty. Then she changed ONE imaginary detail for each group — and the pattern completely flipped. What was it?
---
**The time horizon.** Tell older subjects to imagine a medical breakthrough granting *twenty extra years* — and their choices matched the young, novelty and all. Tell young people to imagine *moving across the country next week* — and they chose like the old: the deepest, most reliable bonds. It was never age, friend. **It was the interval.** Her socioemotional selectivity theory reframes late-life narrowing as the *exploit phase*, executed rationally: with a shorter horizon, unknown acquaintances carry little expected payoff, so older adults prune to their richest relationships — and report *higher* emotional well-being, not loneliness. The shrinking circle isn't decline. **It's the same algorithm every rational agent runs at the end of an interval: cash in your best-known rewards.** One caveat keeps exploration alive at every age, though: the world is a *restless bandit* — payoffs drift, restaurants change chefs, friends move, skills go stale — so some periodic exploring stays necessary forever. And when the new place disappoints, remember: exploration is judged by the portfolio, not the pull. The occasional new favorite pays for all the misses.
:::

## Before you go

So: every repeated choice is a slot machine that pays in coins *and* information; the interval sets the strategy — explore early, exploit late, and Hollywood's sequels confess its horizon; Gittins priced the unknown above the proven; UCB's optimism keeps lifetime regret growing ever slower; adaptive trials turn statistics into mercy; and your grandmother's small, deep circle is optimal play, not surrender. This week, friend: if you're early in *any* interval — new job, new city, new field — deliberately over-sample the unknown. And if the horizon's short somewhere, order the usual with your head held high.

But once you've gathered all these options, friend, a new problem arrives: *putting them in order*. ## Key takeaways

- Repeated choices are multi-armed bandits: every pull is both payoff and data — explore to learn, exploit to earn.
- The interval is everything: explore early when discoveries have a long shelf life, exploit late as the horizon closes — sequels and toddlers both obey.
- Gittins' shock: an untested option can rationally outrank a known 70% winner — uncertainty itself carries a bonus.
- UCB and logarithmic regret: judge options by their plausible best, eliminate only with evidence, and mistakes thin out every year.
- Bandit ethics: adaptive play-the-winner trials could have saved infant lives in the ECMO saga — 50/50 forever has a human cost.
- Carstensen: shrinking circles in old age are rational end-of-interval exploitation — but the world is restless, so never stop exploring entirely.
